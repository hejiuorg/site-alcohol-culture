Time in a cup

From the spring wine of the Book of Songs to the golden goblets of Tang poetry, the ancients wrote seasons, farm work and gatherings into their drinking. This article discusses only what the documents can support, separating text, edition and present-day imagination.

Wine connects farm work, seasons and gatherings. When reading ancient documents, what appears first is often not “what a certain drink tasted like” but “when it was brewed, when it was drunk, and with whom”. Time is therefore not the background of the drink but part of it.

Three kinds of time in a drink

The first is agricultural time: when the crop ripens determines when raw material is available. The second is craft time: how long qu-making, fermentation and ageing take determines when the drink can be consumed. The third is social time: seasons, banquets, partings and reunions determine when the drink is opened.

The three often overlap. Grain after the autumn harvest, wine between winter and spring, a cup raised when the season arrives. Keeping these layers apart while reading avoids equating “wine brewed in spring” directly with any particular product today.

Spring wine and the autumn harvest

In the Book of Songs, “Odes of Bin: Qi Yue”, we read:

In the eighth month they knock down the dates; in the tenth they reap the rice; and make the spirits for the spring, for the benefit of the bushy eyebrows.

The lines place rice harvesting, wine-making and a wish for long life in one sequence of time: dates in the eighth month, rice in the tenth, then spring wine brewed for blessings of long life. The “spring wine” here cannot be directly equated with any specific drink today; what it tells us is that wine held a place in the seasonal and ritual life of the time.

Notably, the poem does not describe aroma or taste. It cares about season, labour and blessing. That shows how early documents recorded wine: with emphasis on “when and why”, not on “what it tasted like”.

Seasons, ritual and shared drinking

In the same poem, wine also appears in banquets and sacrifices: people gather, kill sheep, raise cups and offer blessings. Wine here performs a relational function: it links a family, a village, one generation and the next. The season provides the occasion, ritual provides the form, and wine provides the medium.

This structure of shared drinking recurs in Chinese poetry. It does not require an expensive drink; it requires a suitable occasion, people present and the right time. With that in mind, later poems of parting and poems sent to friends become easier to read for what lies beyond the wine.

Once craft was written down

Jia Sixie's Qimin Yaoshu, from the Northern Wei, records various methods of making and using qu and of brewing. When craft enters a text, experience can be copied, compared and taught. It is not the same as a fixed recipe for a particular region today, but it provides traceable documentary clues for understanding traditional brewing.

From “wine in poetry” to “wine in a manual” is an important shift: the former records feeling and occasion, the latter records steps and proportions. Read together, the two kinds of text show both the cultural place of wine and its technical conditions. A purely literary reading misses the craft; a purely technical one loses the occasion.

Cups in poetry

By the Tang dynasty, wine continued to appear in poetry. In Li Bai's “Qiang Jin Jiu”, the lines “when life is good, enjoy it to the full; do not let the golden goblet stand empty against the moon” put drinking, time and emotion together. This essay quotes those two lines and cites the received text in Complete Tang Poems; no separate excerpt page is created.

Bai Juyi's “Asking Liu” returns wine to a smaller scene: “evening comes and snow is in the air; can you come and drink a cup?” Dusk and approaching snow turn a cup into an invitation and companionship. Both poems write about time, but one is a broad reflection on life and the other a specific evening.

Reading these lines, there is no need to treat the ancients' intake as advice for today. What is more worth noticing is how wine becomes a language for expressing a sense of time: after the harvest, among friends, under the moon, the cup is given meaning beyond drinking. To read the two poems closely, see Li Bai, Drinking Alone by Moonlight and Bai Juyi, Asking Liu.

How to read the documents today

First, confirm the text. Classical works differ between editions, and the same line may be punctuated differently in different editions. Citing a base text or a received edition is more reliable than saying vaguely that “the ancients said”. Second, separate text from interpretation. The line itself is material; “what it shows” is a later judgement, and the two should be stated separately.

Third, mind the context. Wine in a poem may mean rice wine, fruit wine or a local drink, and cannot be mapped directly onto today's yellow wine, baijiu or grape wine. If a style comparison is made, say whether the correspondence is a conjecture or supported by evidence. Between today's yellow wine and ancient “spring wine” there is a distance in name and craft that cannot simply be equated.

Fourth, keep appreciation apart from advice. Reading poetry gives access to emotion and imagery; “how one should drink” is another matter, to be based on modern health and safety knowledge rather than on lines of verse.

Three boundaries for reading documents

This essay offers no collation conclusions and does not judge one edition superior to another; quoted texts follow received editions, and variant readings are not adjudicated. The page does not treat poems as drinking advice: ancient intake, alcohol strength and vessel capacity all differ from today. Nor does it substitute literature for craft history; the details of qu-making and brewing still require specialist sources.

For Time in a cup, begin with the subject it covers, then the sources it draws on, and finish with what it adds to the general account; that order keeps the account grounded.
