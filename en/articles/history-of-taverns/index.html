A history of taverns: from wine shops and inns to bars

The form of a tavern is shaped by cities, transport, taxation and regulation. Comparing Chinese wine houses, European inns and taverns, 19th-century bars and American saloons, this article explains why the same function took different shapes, and how to read an old tavern.

The tavern is an odd space: it sells wine, but it is not only a wine shop. It also provides sociability, information, temporary lodging, employment and entertainment. Different societies distribute these functions across different buildings: the Chinese wine house, the European inn and tavern, the Japanese izakaya, the American saloon and the British pub. The names differ, but the functions overlap.

A history organised only by “places that sell wine” becomes a list of names. A more explanatory approach treats the tavern as a function of four variables: urban density, transport, taxation and monopoly, and regulation and social movements. The same function grows into very different spaces under different conditions.

Argument

The form of a tavern is not a product of “national character” but of institutions and technology. The layered wine trade of Song China came from brewing rights under the wine monopoly; the division between the European inn and tavern came from roads, coaching and franchises; the rise and fall of the American saloon came from railway cities, immigrant politics and the temperance movement; the transformation of the British pub came from licensing law, chain capital and smoking bans.

Comparing taverns should therefore not ask “which country is more traditional” but: who is allowed to sell, where, to whom, at what hours, and how the revenue is taxed. Answer those five questions and the spatial form is largely explained.

Evidence one: proper taverns and foot shops in the Song

Northern Song Bianjing had a clear hierarchy. Dongjing Meng Hua Lu records seventy-two “proper taverns”, with countless “foot shops” below them: the proper taverns had brewing rights and could wholesale, while the foot shops took wine from them and sold at retail. The same text notes that wine shops bound decorated gateways at their doors, traded until the third watch and reopened at the fifth, and sold new wine in season. Flags, lanterns and door decorations served as advertising.

Behind this lay the Song wine monopoly. The production and sale of wine and yeast were brought under official management, and the brewing rights of the proper taverns were part of a system of franchises and taxation rather than free competition. The wine house was therefore not only a restaurant; it was the intersection of urban services, monopoly and local revenue. Inside, seating separated casual customers, banquets and private rooms, making the tavern a good place to observe urban class and spending power.

Evidence two: inns, taverns and coffee houses in Europe

In the European tradition, the inn mainly served travellers with lodging and food; the tavern sold wine and often meals; the alehouse was a smaller household operation. The boundaries shifted: many inns sold wine, and many taverns lodged guests. In the medieval and early modern periods, selling wine required a franchise from a lord or municipality. The English Alehouse Act of 1552 required alehouse keepers to obtain a licence, showing that the tavern was regulated from the start.

In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, coffee houses rose in cities. The first in London is usually dated to 1652, and they became places for information, business and political debate. Taverns and coffee houses overlapped and competed: one leaned toward sociability and relaxation, the other toward reading and argument. In the mid-eighteenth century London had a “gin craze”, in which cheap spirits were linked to poverty and crime; the Gin Act of 1751 tried to tighten sales through licences and taxes. After the Industrial Revolution, swelling cities made the pub a daily space for the working class.

Evidence three: the American saloon and Prohibition

In the nineteenth-century United States, saloons spread with cities and railways. They offered drink, food, gambling, lodging and job information, and were often tied to local politics: immigrant political machines operated through saloons, and saloon keepers acted as brokers. At the same time, the temperance movement saw the saloon as a symbol of social problems, attributing drinking, poverty, domestic violence and immigrant neighbourhoods to it.

The Anti-Saloon League, founded in 1893, pushed local temperance into national politics. In 1919 the Eighteenth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution received enough state approvals and took effect in January 1920, prohibiting the manufacture, sale and transport of intoxicating liquors; the Twenty-First Amendment repealed it in 1933. American Prohibition closed many legal saloons but did not eliminate demand: bootlegging, speakeasies and organised crime filled the gap. The episode shows that law can change the form of the tavern, but social demand does not disappear; it moves elsewhere.

Evidence four: the British pub and the Japanese izakaya

The British pub is often treated as a community space: regulars, familiar seats, a drinks list and opening hours together form a local order. The Beerhouse Act of 1830 lowered the barrier to selling beer and caused a surge in beer houses; in the late twentieth century the pub went through chaining, food service and the 2007 indoor smoking ban in England, and the traditional “small house selling beer” became rarer.

The Japanese izakaya is often traced to the mid-Edo period, when small shops sold wine for customers to drink standing or with simple food, later developing into a format with food and seats. In the late twentieth century, chain izakaya standardised prices, menus and hours and became part of the infrastructure of city nightlife. Placing the pub and the izakaya side by side reveals two paths: one through community and licensing, the other through chains and efficiency.

Counterexamples and limits

First, taverns do not evolve in a straight line. The speakeasy of Prohibition was not a “more backward tavern” but a variant under legal pressure; today's craft beer bar, cocktail bar and pop-up are not “advanced stages” of the pub but parallel choices for different consumers.

Second, taverns are not necessarily declining. Drinking at home and alcohol delivery have taken over part of the social function, but craft beer, cocktails and experience-led spaces are expanding at the same time. Treating “young people drink less” as a single trend ignores the segmentation of consumption.

Third, written records have a perspective bias. Chinese notes mostly record cities and famous houses, while rural wine stalls and roadside sellers are rarely documented; European licensing archives come largely from municipalities and churches and may not cover all informal sellers. Taking famous houses as the whole industry overstates their representativeness.

Fourth, data on tax, licences and opening hours are more reliable than literary description. A “wine flag” in a poem offers an image but cannot establish the number or scale of taverns; accounts, licences and tax records are needed to argue change.
