The spread of distillation: from alchemy to global spirits

Distillation was not invented by one person or one country. Following Alexandria, Arabic alchemy, European medicinal distillation, Caribbean rum, East Asian spirits and 19th-century column stills, this article shows how technique travelled through translation, trade and empire.

Fermentation can make wine, but alcohol strength has a ceiling: as concentration rises, yeast is inhibited. To obtain a stronger drink, alcohol must be separated from the fermented liquid. That is distillation. The principle is simple: heat the mixture, let alcohol vaporise first, then condense and collect it. The difficulty lies in apparatus, materials, fuel and purpose.

The history of distillation is therefore not a single story of “who invented it first” but several routes meeting in different regions: Alexandria in the Hellenistic period, alchemy in the Islamic world, medicinal distillation in medieval Europe, rum in Atlantic trade, East Asian grain spirits, and industrial distillation in the 19th century.

Principles and early apparatus

Distillation relies on the different boiling points of alcohol and water. The earliest devices were mostly pot stills: a heated vessel, rising vapour and a condensing tube. The word alembic comes from Arabic al-anbiq, whose root can be traced further back to Greek. The etymology alone shows technique moving back and forth across the Mediterranean and West Asia.

Fermentation and distillation must be distinguished: the former uses microbes to turn sugar into alcohol, the latter is physical separation. Fermentation can exist on its own; distillation builds on fermented wine.

Alexandria and Arabic alchemy

Around the 1st century, artisans and scholars in Alexandria recorded apparatus for heating, condensing and extracting, mainly for perfumes, medicines and metals. Later, texts from the Arabic-speaking world systematised stills and procedures, with clearer descriptions of vessels appearing in works of the 8th and 9th centuries.

Alchemy sought not wine but “essences”. Once the apparatus and methods matured, however, they could be turned to alcohol. Europe encountered these texts through translation after the 12th century, and distillation entered medical schools and pharmacies.

Europe: from medicine to drinking

Medieval Europe treated distilled spirit as medicine, calling it aqua vitae, the water of life, used to preserve herbs and to treat illness. As the technique spread, drinking overtook medical use. In the 14th and 15th centuries, records of grain distillation appear in Scotland and Ireland; the early forms of whisky were tied to monasteries, farms and local grain.

Whisky later developed clear regulations and appellation systems, but its technical origin is not mysterious: pot distillation, grain fermentation and oak maturation are a traceable combination. What determines flavour is raw material, still shape, maturation environment and blending.

Atlantic trade and rum

In the 17th century, Caribbean sugar plantations used distillation on fermented molasses to produce rum. Rum entered transatlantic trade networks and became a common drink of sailors, colonies and port cities. Its spread was tightly bound to slavery, plantation economies and naval supply, and this history should not be hidden behind flavour narratives.

Rum also shows another side of technological spread: the same distillation method, with different raw materials and social relations, produces entirely different goods.

East Asia: shaojiu, shochu and soju

The origin of Chinese distilled spirits has long been disputed. Li Shizhen wrote in the Ming Bencao Gangmu that “shaojiu is not an ancient method; its method was first created in the Yuan”, a sentence with great influence, yet archaeological and textual research suggests distillation may have appeared earlier or separately in different regions. Treating “introduced in the Yuan” as the only answer is not safe.

The craft of baijiu gradually took shape in the Ming and Qing, as sorghum, qu and fermentation pits formed the basis of today's aroma types. Japanese shochu, Korean soju and Ryukyuan awamori have their own raw materials and distillation traditions, tied to East Asian trade, local agriculture and alcohol taxation; they should not be reduced to variants of Chinese shaojiu.

Industrialisation: column stills and standardisation

In 1831 the column still, or continuous distillation, came into use, making high-efficiency, high-purity alcohol production possible. It changed the cost structure and flavour of spirits: continuous distillation yields a more neutral, purer alcohol, while pot distillation retains more flavour compounds.

Industrialisation also brought standards, taxation and food-safety regulation. Today vodka, rum and some whiskies rely on continuous distillation, while single malt whisky and some brandies still use pot stills. Technological choice directly shapes product style.

Conclusion: technology has no single origin

The story of distillation is an overlay of apparatus, use, trade and regulation. Alchemy supplied vessels and vocabulary, medicine supplied legitimacy, colonial trade supplied raw materials and markets, and the Industrial Revolution supplied efficiency and standards. Every region reinvented the technique within this network.

So instead of asking where distilled spirits first appeared, ask under what conditions distillation moved from medicine to drinking and became a stable industry. The second question better explains why the spirits we drink today look the way they do.
