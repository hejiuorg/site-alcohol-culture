CNFIA: a food-industry body that also trains baijiu judges

The China National Food Industry Association (CNFIA) was approved by the State Council and founded on 29 October 1981 as the self-regulatory industry body of China's food industry, with the stated functions of coordination, planning, liaison, guidance and service. It has a baijiu professional committee and from 1984 has organised the national baijiu judge corps; it is not a regulator, and food safety standards and market enforcement belong to government.

Among Chinese industry bodies connected to alcohol, the China National Food Industry Association occupies an unusual position: it is not a specialist drinks association but one covering the whole food industry, yet it has carried out one very specific piece of baijiu work for more than forty years, the national baijiu judge corps. Understanding the association means seeing both its breadth and its specialism.

What kind of organisation it is

The association's English name is China National Food Industry Association, abbreviated CNFIA. Approved by the State Council, it was founded on 29 October 1981 as the self-regulatory industry body of China's food industry, based in Beijing, with its official site at www.cnfia.cn.

Its functions can be summed up as coordination, planning, liaison, guidance and service. Those five words describe an industry body: linking companies, assisting government and providing industry services, not carrying out administrative enforcement. Food safety standards, production licences and market enforcement belong to government departments.

From 1981

In 1981 China's food industry was recovering and rebuilding. With State Council approval the association was founded as the national food-industry body. Its work has followed the industry since then, extending from planning and coordination to quality standards, technical progress, information and statistics, and international exchange.

In alcohol, its most concrete work is the national baijiu judges. In 1984 the association, through training and examinations, formed a national baijiu judge corps of 34 judges, 11 specially invited judges and 7 expert panel members, and completed the fourth national evaluation of famous and quality liquors. The judge system has continued. In 2024 the first annual meeting of the tenth national baijiu judges was held, and the corps had grown to 473 people.

What it does

First, industry planning and coordination, assisting government in coordinating and planning the food industry and linking companies, local associations and relevant departments.

Second, industry services and information, including industry statistics, information exchange, technical promotion and standards publicity to help food companies understand policy and markets.

Third, baijiu professional work. Through its baijiu professional committee it organises training, examinations and annual meetings for the national judges, giving baijiu sensory evaluation a stable professional corps; this is industry evaluation, not product certification.

Fourth, international exchange and exhibitions, organising industry exchange, trade fairs and international cooperation.

How the judge system works

Selection is not a tasting contest but an examination after concentrated training: candidates study process, aroma types and standards, then evaluate samples blind, including aroma identification and defect recognition. Judge status is managed by cohort, with fresh training and assessment at each change. The point is to turn “knowing how to drink” into a testable professional skill; what is assessed is the person's ability.

The system descends from the 1984 corps: 34 judges, 11 specially invited judges and 7 expert panel members were assembled for the fourth national evaluation of famous and quality liquors. By the tenth cohort in 2024 the corps reached 473 people. The growth also reflects a shift from contest organiser to long-term maintainer of professional competence.

Members and standards

Members are food-industry companies and related units, covering alcohol, beverages, dairy, grain and oil and other categories; the exact membership changes with the industry, so this page does not fix a number.

“Standards” here has two layers. Food safety standards are made and enforced by government; the association works on standards publicity, industry self-discipline and professional evaluation. Sensory evaluation by national baijiu judges is an industry professional activity and is neither an official quality certification nor a market access permit.

Relationship with the reader

For consumers, the association's industry information and professional activities can be a reference for understanding China and baijiu, but when buying, the legal information on the label still matters; see how to choose your first baijiu. A judge's opinion is professional opinion, not proof of every bottle's quality.

For people in the trade, the association's statistics, standards publicity and professional activities are a reference for judging the business environment, but cannot replace a company's own quality control and compliance.

Common misreadings

First, taking the CNFIA for a liquor monopoly or food safety regulator. It does industry-body work; regulation and enforcement belong to government.

Second, taking “national baijiu judge” for a product certification. It evaluates the professional competence of judges and their sensory work, not the issuing of certificates for a bottle.

Third, confusing the CNFIA with the China Alcoholic Drinks Association. The former covers the whole food industry; the latter is a specialist drinks body, and their memberships and functions differ.

Fourth, taking the association's industry opinion as law. Industry opinion can be consulted, but legal requirements follow statutes and standards.

The China National Food Industry Association is an industry body, not a government regulator. It represents food and beverage producers, coordinates industry research and standards work, and engages in policy consultation and trade promotion. Its members and activities are separate from the market supervision system that enforces food safety law. An association standard or position paper should not be mistaken for a mandatory national standard.

CNFIA is best approached through the mandate, then the members, and then any standard it publishes. One check alone will not settle it.
