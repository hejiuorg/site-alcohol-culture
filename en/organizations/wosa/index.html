Wines of South Africa: Taking South African Wine to Export Markets

Wines of South Africa (WoSA) is a non-profit organisation owned by the South African wine industry and funded by levies, dedicated to promoting South African wine in international markets. Its head office is in Stellenbosch, with offices in London, New York and Hong Kong and agencies in Canada, Sweden, the Netherlands and Germany. It does not sell wine and does not certify origin; public material does not record a founding year.

The Cape made its first recorded wine on 2 February 1659, a longer history than most so-called New World countries can claim. Yet on an overseas shelf South African wine is still often filed under a broad category, with regions and styles left unclear. Wines of South Africa (WoSA) exists to close that gap: to translate a diverse, old wine country into a national brand that buyers will try.

A trade body that looks only abroad

WoSA is a non-profit organisation of the South African wine industry whose job is to promote South African wine in key international markets. Its own material is explicit that it operates internationally and not inside South Africa. That is not wordplay. Estates, regions and tourism bodies handle the domestic message, while WoSA's budget, staff and programmes all point at export markets.

The test of its work is therefore not whether South Africans know their own wine better, but whether importers, sommeliers and journalists abroad are willing to put South Africa on a buying list.

Industry-owned, levy-funded

It is owned by the trade rather than by government, and it is funded by levies paid by the industry. Those two sentences fix both its freedom and its constraint: it does not have to answer for every policy goal the way a government department does, but it must show levy payers that promotion works.

The organisation also states plainly that it is not a sales body, has no sales staff and takes no orders. The self-limitation matters. Sales are made by wineries and their importers; WoSA's task is to make the name South Africa easier to accept. Treating it as a free export sales office confuses the two roles.

Members, offices and decisions

Members are South African wine exporters and related bodies who join under a membership scheme; the list and the number change with the trade, and no fixed total is published. A board drawn from the industry and a secretariat run the organisation, with its head office in Stellenbosch.

The overseas footprint shows how resources are allocated: an office in London with two people, one person in New York and one in Hong Kong, plus a full-time person based in Johannesburg for Africa. Canada, Sweden, the Netherlands and Germany are covered through agencies in Toronto, Stockholm, the Netherlands and Baden-Baden. A small team spread across many markets has to choose priorities rather than spread itself evenly.

Brand promotion, information and market access

The first output is brand promotion. WoSA takes a single visual and narrative identity to international fairs, organising national stands, tastings and media work that place wines from different regions inside one South African story.

The second is information and education: websites, press releases, regional maps, e-brochures and online courses form a library for consumers, the trade, journalists and students.

The third is market access support. Exporters receive an importer database, market briefings and itineraries for visiting buyers and journalists, while events such as the International Sommelier Cup help build brand ambassadors.

The fourth is partnership. WoSA works with South African Tourism, industry bodies and government departments to bring wine tourism into the national promotion.

Dates, locations and markets that can be checked

The checkable points include the first winemaking record of 2 February 1659; the head office in Stellenbosch; the fixed presence of two people in London, one in New York and one in Hong Kong; four agency markets in Canada, Sweden, the Netherlands and Germany; and a 2022-2025 strategy that names the United States, China and Angola, Kenya, Uganda, Nigeria and Ghana as growth markets, with the United Kingdom, Germany, the Netherlands, Sweden and Canada as markets to defend.

These figures describe the allocation of effort, not the size of the industry. As for a founding year, public material does not record one clearly, so the field is left empty rather than filled by guesswork.

What it does for the national image

South African wine is heavily export-dependent, and a buyer's impression of a country usually arrives before any knowledge of an estate. WoSA turns the country into a recognisable entrance: a single identity lowers the cost of recognition and spares small wineries the fixed expense of going abroad alone. For medium-sized estates, national stands and collective tastings are a chance to meet importers; for regional bodies, wine promotion lends weight to tourism and investment stories.

The effect is indirect. It adds nothing to any single bottle, but it affects whether a buyer opens that page of the list at all.

Limits and controversies

The first limit is jurisdiction. WoSA is not a regulator: the Wine of Origin scheme is administered by the Wine and Spirit Board, and Sustainable Wine South Africa is an alliance of that board, the Integrated Production of Wine scheme and WoSA. Mistaking WoSA for a certification body misreads its powers.

The second is market concentration. Resources go to a few priority markets, while others depend on agencies and short projects, so coverage is uneven.

The third is structural. Port logistics, electricity supply, drought and disease are not variables that promotion can fix; a national brand can attract attention but cannot keep orders that supply instability drives away.

The fourth is representation and money. An organisation funded by levies will always argue about how resources are shared between large exporters and small estates.

The fifth is policy tension. Alcohol promotion and public health policy pull in different directions, and the more successful the marketing, the more likely the boundary of marketing is questioned.

Common misreadings

Taking WoSA for a government department; for a winery association; for an overseas sales agent; for an origin certification body. Each claim inflates its authority and underrates the actual work, which is to make a place visible.
