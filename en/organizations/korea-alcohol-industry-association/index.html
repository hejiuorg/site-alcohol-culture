Korea Alcohol Industry Association: The Trade Body for Soju and Other Drinks

The Korea Alcohol Industry Association was founded in November 1980 and represents companies making soju, cheongju, whisky and other drinks. It promotes the industry, carries out research and informs the public, but it is not a regulator: licensing, tax and distribution rules belong to government, and its statistics cannot replace official statistics.

Soju is the largest category in the Korean drinks market, but drinks companies do not make soju alone: cheongju, whisky and fruit wines also have their producers. The Korea Alcohol Industry Association is the trade body that brings these companies together. Its work is promotion, research and coordination rather than enforcement. Taking the association for a "Korean drinks authority" is one of the most common misreadings of the Korean drinks market.

What kind of organisation it is

The Korean name is Hanguk Juryu Saneop Hyeophoe, and the English name is usually Korea Alcohol Industry Association, abbreviated KALIA. It was founded in November 1980 and is based in Seoul. It is a trade organisation whose members are mainly drinks manufacturers and sellers.

Membership covers soju, cheongju, whisky and other categories. The association is neither a government department nor a regulator; licensing, tax and distribution rules belong to government, while the association's role is coordination, promotion and information exchange. It can express an industry view, but it cannot issue a licence or impose a penalty in place of government.

From 1980

In November 1980, the Korean drinks industry was expanding and adjusting at the same time. Companies needed a standing platform for industry promotion, market information and public communication, and the association was founded for that purpose.

Since then its work has followed the market: from domestic consumption and the expansion of categories to export promotion, responsible-drinking campaigns and industry image. Its name and offices have been adjusted, and its list of tasks has changed with policy and the market. Because of these changes, a membership list or a set of duties from one year should not be read as the present situation.

What it does: standards, statistics, promotion

First, industry promotion. The association promotes Korean drinks at home and abroad, organising events, publishing material and supporting members' market communication.

Second, research. It collects production, sales and consumption data and publishes an overview of the trade as a reference for members and outsiders. Such data mostly come from member reports, so they are good for reading trends and poor as audited figures for individual companies.

Third, public information. It runs public education on responsible drinking, drinks knowledge and consumption settings, reducing the risks of misuse and excess.

Fourth, coordination. It acts as a channel between member companies and between the trade and government. It can organise industry discussion and form a common position, but a position is not a regulation.

The classification boundaries of Korean drinks: soju, makgeolli, cheongju

Korean drinks are often reduced to "soju", but there are at least three different production lines. Soju is a distilled drink, divided into traditional distilled soju and diluted soju; diluted soju is made by blending neutral spirit with water and flavourings and commonly has about 16 to 17 percent alcohol, while traditional distilled soju is usually stronger. It belongs to the broad distilled category with Japanese shochu and Chinese baijiu, but its raw materials, process and flavour differ.

Makgeolli is a fermented rice wine, unfiltered, cloudy with rice particles and gas, commonly about 6 to 7 percent alcohol; it belongs to the takju, or cloudy wine, category and is not a type of soju. Cheongju is a clear rice wine that is fermented and then filtered, commonly about 13 to 17 percent alcohol, and historically belongs with yakju in the same rice-wine tradition. It resembles Japanese sake in technique, but remains a Korean tradition of its own.

To identify a Korean drink, ask three questions first: is it distilled or fermented? Is the raw material rice or another starch? Is the finished drink clear or cloudy? Relying on the single word "soju" easily mixes fermented and distilled drinks together.

Members and standards

Members are mainly drinks manufacturers and sellers, and the exact composition changes with consolidation in the trade, so a fixed membership figure should not be treated as permanent. Large soju companies, cheongju producers and whisky importers may all appear in the membership system, but membership is not product certification.

Standards here means above all liquor tax, labelling rules and distribution rules. These are made and enforced by government; the association coordinates but does not replace regulation. What a label may say and whether a drink may be sold ultimately depend on the law. The association may promote self-regulation or make proposals, but it cannot issue an "official quality certificate" for a bottle.

Division of work with government

In Korea, liquor licensing and liquor-tax collection are mainly handled by the National Tax Service system, while tax policy belongs to the Ministry of Economy and Finance; food safety, labelling and ingredient rules belong to the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety; imports also involve customs and tariff procedures. Different stages are handled by different bodies, and specific powers should be checked against official sources.

The association stands outside these bodies: it gathers industry opinion, organises communication and provides material, but it does not issue licences or decide tax burdens. Treating its promotional material as legal text, or its industry position as a regulatory decision, overstates its authority. For readers, the reliable route is still the legal information on the label and the published rules of the competent authorities.

How to read the association's material

When reading an association statistic or trade report, check four dimensions first: the measurement basis, shipment or retail; the unit, value or volume; the coverage, member companies or the whole trade; and the period, annual, quarterly or monthly. Different dimensions can lead to completely different conclusions.

The strength of association data is that it shows trade trends and category structure; the weakness is that it may not cover every producer and may not be externally audited. It is therefore suitable for directional questions such as where soju sits within Korean drinks or whether exports are growing, and unsuitable for precise questions such as a brand's market share. When citing it, state the source and the year, and avoid presenting member-reported figures as official statistics.

What it means for readers

For consumers, the association's material is one way into Korean drinks, but a purchase should still be checked against the legal information on the label, and readers may consult How to Start Tasting. Soju is the representative distilled drink of soju, but Korean soju is not the same as Japanese shochu or Chinese baijiu.

For people in the trade, the association's statistics and positions are a reference for reading the market, but they are not proof of any one company's quality. The company, the ingredients and the process are the specific variables. For researchers, the association website can be cross-checked with the Encyclopedia of Korean Culture and KOTRA directories, but on regulatory detail the texts of the competent authorities remain the final reference.

Common misreadings

First, taking the association as a government regulator. Licensing, tax and distribution rules belong to government. Second, taking it as a soju-only organisation. Its members cover several drinks categories. Third, confusing Korean soju with Japanese shochu. Both are distilled, but their ingredients, processes and flavours differ. Fourth, taking the association's promotion as a guarantee of quality. Promotion speaks for the trade as a whole, not for every bottle. Fifth, taking makgeolli as a type of soju. Makgeolli is a fermented cloudy wine; soju is distilled. Sixth, equating Korean cheongju with Japanese sake. The techniques are close, but the traditions and product systems are separate. Seventh, taking member-reported association data as official statistics. The basis, coverage and degree of audit all differ.
