FEV: gathering the voice of Spanish wineries

The Federación Española del Vino (FEV), founded in 1978, is Spain's national private non-profit winery association, representing more than 950 wineries and 17 associations whose combined output is about 83% of the value of Spanish bottled wine. It works on policy, industry coordination, market observation and sustainability certification, and is not a regulator; its statutes are deposited with the Spanish Ministry of Labour and Social Economy, with the latest amendment filed on 18 November 2020.

Spain has one of the largest areas under vines in the world, yet its wineries are highly fragmented: international brands in Rioja and Ribera del Duero sit beside thousands of small estates that sell only in their own province. Turning that into an industry that can speak in Madrid and Brussels at the same time needs a national association. The Federación Española del Vino (FEV) fills that role.

Founding and association status

The FEV was founded in 1978. Its legal form is a private non-profit association. Its statutes are deposited with the register of employer and trade-union statutes at the Spanish Ministry of Labour and Social Economy, and the latest amendment was filed on 18 November 2020. The status matters: it is built from voluntary members, runs on subscriptions and project funding, and is not a statutory body.

Its remit covers still wine, sparkling wine, fortified wine, aromatised wine, grape must and vinegar. It describes itself as the most representative private organisation of the Spanish winery sector, at home and abroad. It does not issue alcohol licences or draw the boundaries of protected designations.

The general assembly and three-year terms

The sovereign body is the general assembly of all members, whose decisions bind the membership under the statutes. The president is elected by the assembly every three years, and candidates must previously have served as first vice-president; after the presidential term, the outgoing president becomes second vice-president, also for three years.

Since 10 April 2024 the president has been Pedro Ferrer Noguer of Ferrer Wines, who is also honorary president of Freixenet. An executive commission handles ordinary management, made up of the president, vice-presidents and members, and serving until the 2027 assembly. Director General José Luis Benítez Robredo executes decisions and runs the technical team. The president, vice-presidents and members are unpaid, as the association's transparency page states.

Policy, market observatory and sustainability certification

The first daily task is policy representation. The association takes part in rule-making at EU, international, Spanish central-government and regional level, on labelling, taxation, appellation rules, the common agricultural policy and market access. It also proposes amendments to draft legislation.

The second task is market information. The association helped found the Spanish wine market observatory (OEMV), which brings production, stock, price and export data together. The third is sustainability certification: in 2015 the association launched the Sustainable Wineries for Climate Protection (SWfCP) seal for wineries, and in 2022 announced coverage of social, economic and governance criteria.

The fourth is promotion and responsible drinking. The association takes part in the Wine in Moderation programme, which stresses moderate consumption by adults, and supports the promotion of Spanish wine abroad. The certification and promotion are run by the association or its partners, but certification remains voluntary.

The industry figures the association publishes

The association's website says it represents more than 950 wineries and 17 associations, and that member wineries together account for about 83% of the value of Spanish bottled wine. That share refers to bottled wine value, not volume or vineyard area, and should not be quoted as if it were a production share.

The sector figures it publishes show the scale: 913,695 hectares of vineyard in 2023, the largest vineyard area in the world; average annual production of 32.4 million hectolitres, the third largest in the world; a wine sector worth 1.9% of Spanish GDP, with more than EUR 20.33 billion in gross value added and 363,980 jobs; export value of EUR 2.988 billion in 2023, sold in 189 countries; and 97 protected designations covering quality wines. The association also lists EUR 517,475.71 of public funding received in 2025, item by item, on its transparency page.

Position in the Spanish wine map

Wine governance in Spain is layered: the EU sets market rules, the central government sets wine law and labelling, regional governments and designation authorities set local rules, and wineries produce. The FEV sits at the representation layer, gathering winery views and carrying them into those decision-making rooms.

That also explains its emphasis on bottled wine. Spain has long been one of the world's largest exporters of bulk wine, which is low-priced and thin-margin, and its interests do not always align with those of bottled and branded wine. The FEV's membership and its 83% value measure both lean towards bottled, branded producers. Anyone trying to understand the wine industry of Spain needs to keep the bulk and bottled lines apart.

Who it represents, who it does not

The FEV represents wineries, meaning the companies that make and bottle wine, not all grape growers. Growers' interests are represented separately by cooperatives and grower organisations, and the two sides do not always agree on grape prices. Treating the FEV as the single voice of Spanish grape growing overstates its coverage.

A second limit is size distribution. More than 950 members sounds large, but Spain has several thousand wineries, and members tend to be medium and large estates with export capacity. Third, SWfCP is the association's own sustainability certification, not a mandatory appellation rule; it can help a winery demonstrate environmental performance, but it does not replace the production rules of a protected designation. Fourth, public funding and policy advocacy coexist, and readers assessing the association's positions should keep the funding in view.

Points easily confused

  • Taking the FEV for a government body. It is a private non-profit association; law and appellation rules are made by government and designation authorities.
  • Reading 83% as a volume share. The website speaks of the value of bottled wine.
  • Equating Spanish wine with Rioja alone. Spain has 97 protected designations, with wide differences in varieties and styles.
  • Taking SWfCP for a statutory appellation certification. It is a voluntary sustainability certification promoted by the association.
  • Treating the membership figure as fixed. Lists and numbers change with consolidation, and the latest data on the website should be used.
