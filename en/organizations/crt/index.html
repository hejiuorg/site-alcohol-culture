Tequila Regulatory Council: Who Certifies That a Bottle of Tequila Is Genuine

The Tequila Regulatory Council (CRT) was founded in Mexico on 17 May 1994 as a civil, non-profit organisation based in Zapopan, Jalisco. It brings agave growers, tequila producers, bottlers, distributors and government agencies into one body, inspects and certifies compliance with NOM-006-SCFI-2012, and defends the Tequila appellation in Mexico and abroad. It is accredited and approved by Mexican bodies, but it is not a government department.

Tequila was among the first Mexican spirits to win appellation protection, and it is one of the most imitated. Who confirms that a bottle really comes from the statutory region and was made to the standard? In Mexico, much of that work is done by an industry body: the Tequila Regulatory Council (Consejo Regulador del Tequila, CRT).

17 May 1994 and civil non-profit status

The CRT was founded on 17 May 1994 as a civil, non-profit organisation. It is not part of the Mexican government; it is a conformity assessment body built by the different actors in the trade. Its office is at Av. Patria 723, Jardines de Guadalupe, C.P. 45030, Zapopan, Jalisco.

That position has to be kept separate from government functions. The Tequila appellation was declared by the Mexican federal state, and the product standard NOM-006-SCFI-2012 is an official Mexican standard. The CRT's job is to inspect, test and certify against those rules and to defend the appellation. It can issue certificates, but it cannot rewrite the standard or redraw the region.

Full-chain members and technical committees

The CRT's members cover the whole agave-tequila chain: agave growers, tequila producers, bottlers, distributors and government agencies. That full-chain structure makes it more than a simple trade association; it is a coordination table where growing, production, distribution and regulation sit together.

In governance, a board of directors includes a president, vice-presidents, a treasurer and a secretary. The website listed Aurelio López Rocha as president and Ramón González Figueroa as general director in 2026. Below the board sit technical committees for inspection, certification, standardisation, external affairs, finance, agronomy, honour and impartiality, and strategic planning. Each committee is chaired by a company representative, with the secretariat executing. The design keeps rule interpretation from resting with management alone.

Inspection, certification, laboratory and traceability

The core output is conformity assessment. As an inspection unit, testing laboratory and certification body, the CRT carries out on-site inspections, sampling and document reviews of tequila producers, bottlers and export consignments; products that meet NOM-006-SCFI-2012 can receive a certificate. It also acts as a greenhouse-gas verification body, giving distilleries third-party assessment of environmental data.

Its authority comes from external accreditation. The Mexican Accreditation Entity (EMA) grants its inspection, testing, certification and greenhouse-gas verification accreditations; the General Bureau of Standards of the Ministry of Economy approves its activities; and the federal environmental protection agency, PROFEPA, approves its greenhouse-gas verification. In 1999 it received recognition from Spain's AENOR and Mexico's ANCE. In 2023 it obtained the Pro-Integrity distinction promoted by Jalisco state and federal anti-corruption bodies.

Another output is information. The CRT keeps a register of brands and companies, publishes the region, the standard and the certification process, and provides production, price and stock information to the chain. For consumers, the CRT number on a bottle and the "100% blue agave" mark are entry points into that traceability chain.

Numbers in the certification system

The Tequila appellation declaration was published in Mexico's Official Gazette on 9 December 1974. The protected area covers 181 municipalities in five states: 125 in Jalisco, 30 in Michoacán, 7 in Guanajuato, 8 in Nayarit and 11 in Tamaulipas. Only blue agave (Agave tequilana Weber) may be used.

CRT certification covers production, bottling and export, with scopes including NOM-006-SCFI-2012, NMX-V-049-NORMEX-2004 and section 9 of NOM-142-SSA1/SCFI-2014. The appellation has also obtained protection or registration in many markets, including the European Union and Canada in 1979, China in 2006 and 2014, the United States in 2017, the United Kingdom in 2019 and the United Arab Emirates in 2025. The CRT was founded in 1994, twenty years after the declaration, which shows the order of events: appellation first, regulator later.

The boundaries of the Tequila appellation

The appellation limits both the place and the raw material. Blue agave must be grown inside the region, and the spirit must go through the required fermentation, distillation and ageing there. A similar spirit made outside the boundary cannot be called Tequila, even with the same process. This line separates Tequila from other agave spirits such as mezcal.

The region itself is not uniform. The highlands (Los Altos) and the lowlands (Valles) of Jalisco differ in altitude, soil and ripeness, and the styles follow. Readers can start with the varieties and farming of agave, then look at the categories and ageing levels of tequila, and finally place it on the map of Mexico.

The controversy over industry self-governance

The first argument is conflict of interest. The CRT is made up of industry members and certifies industry products; critics ask how a body funded, staffed and governed by the trade can be strict with offenders. Supporters point to external accreditation and the separation of powers: certification decisions are taken by technical committees, accreditors audit regularly, and government keeps the power to set the standard.

The second argument is the difference between compliance and quality. CRT certification says a product meets the standard, not that it tastes good. A compliant bottle can be dull, and a distinctive bottle can have imperfect paperwork. The third is the agave cycle. Agave takes years to mature and prices swing sharply with supply and demand; an industry body can coordinate information but can hardly smooth the cycle. The fourth is representativeness: large producers carry more weight on the committees, and the interests of small distilleries and growers may not receive equal attention.

Common misreadings

  • Taking the CRT for a government agency. It is a civil non-profit body; the standard and the appellation declaration come from government.
  • Taking CRT certification as a flavour rating. It certifies compliance, not quality.
  • Using Tequila as the name for every agave spirit. Mezcal, Raicilla and others have their own regions and rules.
  • Reading "100% blue agave" as an ageing grade. The first describes the raw material, the second the time in cask.
  • Treating 1994 as the start of appellation protection. The declaration dates from 1974; the CRT came later as its enforcement body.
