Mid-Autumn moon wine

The 15th day of the eighth lunar month is the Mid-Autumn Festival, when moon gazing, moon worship, eating mooncakes and drinking are often placed in the same evening. Wine has at least four roles in this custom: an offering to the moon, a drink at the reunion meal, a medium through which poets address the moon, and new wine sought after in the market before the festival. Separating these four layers shows which parts are documentary records and which are later explanations.

Time and place

Mid-Autumn falls on the 15th day of the eighth lunar month, though some places keep it on the 16th; names include Moon Eve, Autumn Festival and Reunion Festival. It is observed in China and in different parts of the East Asian cultural sphere, but ritual details vary greatly: some places centre on family moon worship, some on taverns and moon viewing, and some on mooncakes as gifts. Treating one local practice as a nationwide custom hides this variation.

The date itself is a reminder: Mid-Autumn is a point in the middle of autumn, when harvest, weather and the moon all draw concentrated attention. Wine enters this point not only as festivity but also in relation to new grain, new wine and the ritual calendar.

Ritual and wine

Traditional moon worship usually sets an offering table in a courtyard or on a terrace, with mooncakes, fruit and wine, then burns incense, performs a salute and shares the offerings. Who leads the rite, how the table is arranged and how many times wine is poured all differ by place; some records have women leading, others elders. Here wine is first something offered, and only second something drunk.

The family meal puts wine back on the dining table. Mid-Autumn dishes often follow seasonal produce, and the wine varies with region and household: rice wine, yellow wine, baijiu and fruit wine may all appear. In recent years lower-alcohol drinks, sparkling wine and alcohol-free options have entered the Mid-Autumn table, showing that the form of a custom changes with consumption habits.

The roles of wine

First, an offering. Wine stands beside mooncakes and fruit as a gift to the moon; after the offering it is shared, completing a shift from sacred to communal.

Second, a medium of reunion. Mid-Autumn stresses the family being together, and raising a glass confirms that we are at the same table. It need not be expensive; the shared moment matters.

Third, a literary image. Moonlight and wine have long been placed side by side in Chinese poetry; Drinking Alone under the Moon turns the moon, the shadow and wine into one person's dialogue, while later Mid-Autumn poems turn that solitude into longing and good wishes. Poetry helps explain feeling, but a line of verse is not a record of custom.

Fourth, a market rhythm. The Northern Song Dongjing Menghualu records that in Bianjing, before Mid-Autumn, every shop sold new wine; people competed to drink, and by around midday the taverns had none left. This material shows Mid-Autumn as a point in brewing and sales, not only a festival inside the home.

Historical origins

Mid-Autumn gradually became a major nationwide festival around the Tang and Song. The Tang already had many poems on moon viewing; the Northern Song scene in Bianjing is recorded in concrete detail in the Dongjing Menghualu, including new wine, crabs, pomegranates and taverns for moon viewing. In 1076, Su Shi drank through the Mid-Autumn night and wrote the line asking when the bright moon would appear and raising his cup to the blue sky. From the Ming and Qing onward, mooncakes and the idea of reunion became more tightly joined, and in modern times gift-giving, family meals and holiday travel have developed side by side.

It is important to note that mooncakes were not bound to Mid-Autumn from the beginning. Researchers point out that mooncakes were not originally a Mid-Autumn festival food and only later became a seasonal food. Similar layering appears in osmanthus wine, the story of Chang'e and moon-viewing poetry: the versions familiar today are often the result of long accumulation.

Contemporary changes

A city Mid-Autumn today commonly involves a family meal, a gathering of friends, company gift boxes and a short trip. There are more drink choices than in the past: traditional yellow wine and baijiu remain, while beer, wine, fruit wine and sugar-free drinks also reach the table. As health awareness grows, alcohol-free and low-alcohol alternatives are increasingly common.

Commercialisation has also shifted the centre of gravity. Gift packaging, limited releases and Mid-Autumn-only marketing turn the season into a sales point; this is not necessarily bad, but readers should keep advertising language apart from documentary records. Another change is safety: designated drivers, public transport and the rule of not driving after drinking have become basic norms of city life.

Disputes and cautions

First, do not treat a famous literary line as historical fact. The line about Wu Gang bringing out osmanthus wine comes from modern poetry, not from a record of ancient Mid-Autumn ritual; cite the source and the genre when quoting it.

Second, osmanthus wine recipes are not uniform. Places differ in using osmanthus, rice wine, baijiu or sugar-preserved osmanthus, and no single recipe should be called the only ancient method.

Third, keep worship and drinking separate. Offering wine is a ritual act; whether it is drunk, and by whom, depends on family and local practice. Treating an offering as a rule that everyone must drink is over-reading.

Fourth, act within limits. Mid-Autumn meals often involve alcohol, and minors, pregnant people, those taking medication and anyone who must drive should avoid drinking; this is not spoiling the mood but placing custom back within real conditions.
