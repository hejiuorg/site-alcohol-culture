Housewarming wine

Housewarming wine is the meal and drink offered after a new home is finished or a family moves in; in the north it is often called “warming the pot” or “searing the pot bottom”, in the south “entering the household”. It confirms the joy of settling in, the neighbourhood relationship and social obligations, and raises real questions about pressure to drink, display and indoor pollution.

Moving house is a very concrete business: boxes, furniture, keys, a new address. Housewarming wine turns it into a relationship: relatives and friends are invited to see the new home, eat a meal and drink a cup, which amounts to telling everyone “I have settled here”. It has no unified procedure like a wedding and no single honoree like a birthday banquet; it is a combination of local habit and family circumstance.

Time and place

There is no national date. A family usually chooses one, and those who care about such things consult an almanac or ask someone to pick an auspicious day; others simply choose a convenient weekend. The meal usually comes after moving in, though a few places hold a “warming” before formal occupation. City families often combine it with a wedding or a baby's full-month feast; rural families are more likely to hold a separate banquet.

Names vary markedly. Many northern places call it “warming the pot” (wenguo) or “searing the pot bottom” (liao guodi), stressing the idea of giving the new stove a fire and making the house “hot”; the south more often says “entering the household” (ruhuo) or “housewarming banquet”. In the northeast, “searing the pot bottom” is often tied to a hotpot or stew; in Shandong and Hebei, “warming the pot” involves guests bringing gifts. These expressions are real, but none is universal.

Three different meals should also be kept apart: the “ridge-beam wine” during building, the “completion wine” when the house is finished, and the “housewarming wine” after moving in. They fall at different times and involve different guests and rites. Today many people use the terms interchangeably, but anyone discussing the custom should first ask which stage is meant.

Historical sources

Ancient China had a developed body of custom around dwellings. The Book of Songs, Xiaoya, Sigan, describes building a house and settling in, touching on chambers, sleeping quarters and the continuation of the family; a finished house was linked early on to family order. Later, ridge-beam raising involved choosing a time, hanging red cloth and reciting auspicious words; completion meant thanking the craftsmen and feasting; moving in meant reporting to the ancestors and settling the household gods. Local gazetteers and custom records show many variations.

Housewarming wine is not a rite laid down in a ritual book. It grew out of the everyday sequence of building, completing and moving, as a habit of hospitality. Its core is not wine but “showing the door”: relatives and friends visit the new home once, and the relationship is confirmed. Wine merely makes the meal feel festive. To call it a fixed ancient rite overstates the documentary evidence; to call it a purely modern invention ignores local memory.

Procedure and vessels

Common steps include choosing a date, cleaning the new home, pasting couplets or the character for fortune, moving in large items, placing the bed and stove, preparing food, welcoming guests, toasting, showing guests around, and seeing them off with a return gift. Some regions have customs such as “the eldest enters first”, “carrying rice and water through the door”, or “leaving the lights on for several nights”, for food and clothing and light. These details differ locally and should not be treated as a national standard.

For vessels, traditional households use pots and cups; northern village banquets often pair a large bottle of baijiu with small cups, while some southern places warm yellow wine in a pot. Modern city homes more often open a bottle, pour and let people help themselves; the vessels are simpler, and the sense of ritual rests on the host's first toast. If hosting at home, prepare enough glasses and drinking water, and alcohol-free options.

Wine and food

Housewarming food carries obvious regional and symbolic meanings. The north often eats dumplings, for reunion and the crossing of a threshold; parts of the south eat tangyuan and niangao, for reunion and rising step by step; fish is common almost everywhere, for surplus. In the northeast, “searing the pot bottom” often begins with a hotpot or stew, with guests sitting around it as the new stove is lit. The dishes need not be expensive, but they should be hot and plentiful.

Drink choice follows the scale of the meal: formal banquets often use baijiu, family meals with elders often use yellow wine or rice wine, and younger households may offer beer, sparkling wine or alcohol-free drinks. For pairing, strong-aroma baijiu suits heavy meat dishes, yellow wine suits stews and river fish, and sparkling wine suits cold dishes and the opening of a meal. These are common pairings, not rules.

Change and contemporary practice

Three changes are visible. First, the venue moves outward: more families hold the meal in a hotel or restaurant, avoiding the cleaning and space pressure of hosting at home. Second, the scale shrinks: city nuclear families invite only close relatives and neighbours, while friends help on moving day and gather separately later. Third, gifts change: from bedding, pots, rice and flour to red envelopes, plants, household goods, or direct transfers.

At the same time, a lighter “housewarming party” has appeared: no formal banquet, just drinks and snacks, guests dropping in and out. Its function resembles the traditional meal, letting the new home be seen and blessed, but with less ritual intensity and less burden on the host.

Disputes and health boundaries

First, urging drink. A housewarming is a happy occasion, but the logic of “if you don't drink you lose face” puts guests in an awkward position. The host should offer tea and alcohol-free drinks and not treat drinking as a duty.

Second, drinking and driving. Moving day involves travel, parking and carrying things, and driving after drinking is riskier. Arrange a driver or an overnight stay in advance; it is more useful than an apology later.

Third, indoor pollution. The folk saying that “drinking warms the house and removes formaldehyde” has no scientific basis. Alcohol cannot remove formaldehyde; ventilation, testing and treatment are the proper responses. Drinking heavily in a newly decorated home only adds to the body's burden.

Fourth, noise and neighbours. A home banquet easily disturbs neighbours, especially at night. Giving notice, keeping the volume down and clearing rubbish matter more than the wine.

Fifth, social comparison. The scale of the banquet and the return gifts can create pressure. Keep the budget within your means and put the emphasis on gathering, not on the number of tables or the price of wine.

Common misreadings

First, confusing housewarming wine with wedding or birthday wine. Its subject is the new home and settling in, not a couple or an elder.

Second, assuming “searing the pot bottom” is universal. It is a regional phrase and practice.

Third, believing wine removes formaldehyde. This is an unfounded folk claim.

Fourth, assuming an auspicious date is required. Date selection is a local habit, not a legal or ritual requirement.

Fifth, assuming not holding a banquet is rude. Many families choose a simple or no banquet, and relationships do not suffer.
