Harvest wine and grape-harvest customs

Harvest wine is the drink of thanksgiving, new-taste tasting and village meals after the autumn harvest; in European vineyard regions the same impulse continues as the grape harvest festival. This entry compares Chinese and European forms of harvest drinking, their timing, vessels, food and modern tourist packaging.

Harvest wine is not a single drink but a kind of occasion: after grain is stored, fruit picked and grapes pressed, people use the wine of the new harvest, or wine already laid down, to thank heaven and earth, the ancestors or the neighbours. In China this belongs to the rhythm of autumn harvest and “tasting the new”; in European vineyard regions it belongs to the grape harvest and the village festival. Both are called harvest, but their rites, vessels and foods are not the same.

Time and place

In China, harvest drinking clusters after the autumn harvest, near Mid-Autumn and the autumn community rites. The north harvests millet, wheat and sorghum; the south harvests rice; timing depends on crop and latitude, so there is no fixed date for “harvest wine”. Some places offer the new crop to the ancestors before the family meal; some hold community rites with shared meat and drink; ethnic minority harvest festivals follow their own calendars and traditions, and should not be lumped together.

In European vineyard regions, the harvest usually runs from late summer into autumn. Many villages in France, Italy, Spain and Germany hold grape harvest festivals with picking, pressing demonstrations, parades, markets and tasting. The date depends on the ripeness of the local grapes and changes from year to year; the scale ranges from a village gathering to a large tourist event.

Historical sources

In Chinese texts, the link between harvest and wine is early. The Book of Songs, Qi Yue, says that in the tenth month rice is harvested and spring wine is made, placing harvesting, winemaking and a blessing for long life in the same agricultural rhythm. Later autumn rites and new-taste offerings institutionalised the link: the new grain and new wine were presented first, then everyone ate and drank together. Wine was the medium for “telling the ancestors what this year's harvest was like”.

European grape harvest festivals are interwoven with the history of the wine industry. In the Middle Ages, monasteries, guilds and local lords were deeply involved in vine growing and wine taxes; the harvest was collective village labour and also a moment of reckoning, celebration and religious thanksgiving. In the modern period, wine tourism added performances, markets and branding. Reliable records rarely show exactly which village began its festival in which year, and such details should not be invented.

Procedure and vessels

A common Chinese village sequence is: finish the harvest, dry and store the grain, set up offerings to thank the gods or ancestors, hold a village meal, honour elders and hired helpers, and share new rice and new flour. Wine is first offered and then drunk: poured for the gods and ancestors, then shared. Vessels are pots, bowls and cups; better-off households use pewter or porcelain pots, while large communal meals use big pots poured out for everyone, where sufficiency and liveliness matter more than refinement.

European grape harvest festivals lean toward display: early picking, carrying baskets of grapes, pressing on site, tasting the new wine, with music, parades and stalls. Vessels include harvest baskets, presses, oak barrels and tasting glasses; different regions emphasise their own traditional containers and methods. It is worth saying that “new wine” at a festival is not finished wine: much still has to ferment and mature before release.

Wine and food

Chinese harvest tables centre on new rice, new wheat, pork, chicken, fish and seasonal vegetables; the north may serve dumplings and wheat foods, the south rice and rice cakes. The drink is usually rice wine, yellow wine or a local baijiu, and the point is “new” and “plentiful” rather than fine tasting notes. The sweetness and grain aroma of the wine answer the freshly harvested grain.

European harvest food is closer to everyday regional eating: bread, cheese, sausages, stews and roast meat, with the wine of the year or recent years. Pairings differ between coast and inland, mountain and plain. Festival tasting is usually in small quantities and across several wines, with the emphasis on trying the new and talking, not on drinking to excess.

Change and contemporary practice

Chinese harvest wine is changing in two directions. On one hand, mechanisation and larger farms mean the whole village no longer needs to exchange labour, and communal meals have shrunk; on the other, local tourism and “harvest festival” events put harvest wine back on stage as a display of farming culture. In recent years various places have established a farmers' harvest festival, often linked to the autumn equinox, but activities are arranged locally and wine is only one element.

European grape harvest festivals have become visibly tourist-oriented. Open days at wineries, harvest experiences, concerts and markets package an agricultural rhythm as a purchasable experience. The benefit is that city people learn where wine comes from; the problem is that a performed harvest can obscure the real intensity of labour and seasonal pressure. Climate change is also shifting harvest dates, so claims about “traditional dates” should be made cautiously, without fixed years.

Disputes and health boundaries

First, festivals and excessive drinking. Harvest means relaxation, but a celebration easily turns into pressure to drink. Provide plenty of water, food and alcohol-free options, and arrange a driver.

Second, drinking and driving. Rural roads are poorly lit and narrow, and the risk after a festival is higher. No amount of drinking is compatible with driving.

Third, food safety. Home-made new wine that has not finished fermenting, or is kept in unclean containers, can spoil or build up excess gas; “traditional method” is no substitute for basic hygiene.

Fourth, labour and fairness. Behind the joy of a grape harvest are long hours of work for pickers; a discussion of regional culture should not erase their situation.

Fifth, commercialisation. Turning harvest wine entirely into tickets and merchandise weakens its meaning as community reciprocity.

Common misreadings

First, assuming harvest wine is one fixed drink. It is a kind of occasion and a habit of use, not a recipe.

Second, assuming a grape harvest festival means winemaking is finished. Picking is only the beginning; fermentation and maturation follow.

Third, equating Chinese harvest wine with grape wine. Traditional Chinese harvest drinking is mostly grain or rice wine; grape wine is another lineage.

Fourth, assuming “tasting the new” falls on the same day everywhere. Crops and climates differ, so dates differ.

Fifth, assuming European harvest festivals are ancient and unchanging. Many are products of modern tourism and regional promotion.
