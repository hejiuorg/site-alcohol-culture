Birthday wine

Birthday wine is the honouring drink at birthday and milestone banquets, combining blessing, thanks and family gathering. This entry covers timing, ritual sequence, documentary sources and modern change, with notes on urging drink and health.

Birthday wine is the wine of birthday and milestone banquets. Its point is not drinking but honouring: younger relatives raise a cup to their elders and place blessing, thanks and family order on one table. It belongs to the life rites and to family gatherings, and the wine is a carrier of the blessing rather than the centre of the meal.

The term also has a literal sense: wine specially set aside for a birthday. It can be a family's own aged stock or a bought gift bottle, expensive or plain. Whether a banquet is “proper” depends on seating, forms of address and the order of toasts, not on the price of the wine.

Time and place

There are two kinds of occasion: the annual birthday and the “round” birthdays at sixty, seventy, eighty and ninety. Sixty is called the flower-fruit year because the sexagenary cycle repeats every sixty years. In some regions, seventy-three and eighty-four are treated as difficult years, linked to the ages at which Confucius and Mencius died, and families may hold the celebration a year early or late. Dates usually follow the lunar calendar, though city families may use the Gregorian date.

“Celebrate nine, not ten” is a widespread rule: the sixtieth is held at fifty-nine, the seventieth at sixty-nine, because “nine” sounds like “long” and “ten” suggests fullness and decline. Other sayings, such as “nine for men, ten for women” or “no three for men, no four for women”, vary from place to place.

Regional differences are clear. The north emphasises wheat foods and peach-shaped buns, made of dough, dyed pink and filled with bean paste. The south emphasises longevity noodles, cakes and banquet dishes; in Fujian and Taiwan, pig's trotters with thin noodles are common, symbolising long life. Coastal and inland choices of wine also differ. A family's means and attitudes matter more than “local tradition” in deciding whether and how large to hold the feast.

Sequence and vessels

Common steps include fixing the date and scale, sending invitations, setting up the birthday hall, arranging peach buns and noodles, bowing or presenting flowers, a speech by the elder, cutting a cake or sharing buns, toasting at the table, group photographs and return gifts. A traditional hall displays a picture of the god of longevity, often the Old Man of the South Pole, with the character for longevity, couplets and screens; modern versions use a backdrop and flowers.

Toasts usually begin with the younger generation, first to the birthday person and then in order of seniority. In some places the elder returns a toast. Vessels need not be precious but should form a set: jug, cups and tray together; the cup is held with both hands and slightly lower than the elder's, the most common gesture. This simplified the order of respect in the village drinking rite.

The choice of drink changes with region and generation: yellow wine and rice wine suit a gentle banquet, and Shaoxing jiafan or huadiao are often served; baijiu is common at formal northern occasions; beer and low-alcohol drinks are more popular with younger families. Alcohol-free options are increasingly offered, especially when the elder has a chronic illness or is taking medication.

Symbolic meaning

First, blessing. Raising a cup and saying “health and long life” turns an abstract wish into a gesture. Second, thanks. The elder and the family thank relatives and friends for years of support. Third, order. Who toasts first and who follows expresses seniority and closeness. Fourth, memory. Birthday banquets are occasions for family photographs and old stories, and wine helps people relax and talk.

The objects each carry a meaning: noodles for length, peach buns for longevity, cake for height, wine for duration. Together they form a symbolic system about time and life. The Book of Songs, in the Bin Feng section, says “we make this spring wine, to prolong the eyebrow's years”, one of the earliest literary expressions of the link between spring wine and long life.

Historical change

In early texts, wine for longevity appears as a blessing. The Book of Songs line about spring wine and the “eyebrow's years” shows the idea in the pre-Qin period, while the Xiaoya poem “ten thousand years without end” is a banquet blessing. But a poem's blessing is not the same as a later birthday banquet: the first is literary expression, the second a rite formed in local society over centuries.

The Tang saw an institutional birthday celebration. In the seventeenth year of the Kaiyuan era (729), Emperor Xuanzong made his birthday, the fifth day of the eighth month, the “Thousand Autumns Festival”, with officials offering wine and congratulations; it was later renamed the “Heavenly Longevity Festival”. In the Song, city restaurants hosted banquets, and the Bianjing establishments recorded in Dongjing Meng Hua Lu were already venues for birthdays, gatherings and commercial service. The Qing court pushed birthday banquets to an extreme: the Kangxi emperor's sixtieth birthday in 1713 and the Qianlong emperor's fiftieth year of reign in 1785 both featured “thousand elders banquets” for thousands of elderly guests.

Folk birthday banquets took their standard form in the Ming and Qing: hall, screens, couplets, peach buns and noodles, with the sixtieth, seventieth and eightieth most important. In modern times, hotels, photography and travel turned the celebration from a family affair into a purchasable service.

Contemporary disputes

First, health and pressure. Elderly people may have chronic illness, cognitive decline or medication, and forced drinking increases the risk of falls, low blood sugar, blood-pressure swings and drug interactions; tea or low-alcohol drinks should be available. Second, waste. Large banquets waste food and add to the burden of gift exchange. Third, seating disputes. Seating involves family relationships, and poor handling can cause conflict; it should be discussed in advance.

Fourth, commercialisation. Turning a birthday into a consumption contest weakens the value of company. Fifth, distance. When children work in another city, the banquet may become a video call or a paid event, and how to give real company is harder than arranging display. Sixth, gender and seniority. Traditional banquets centred on male elders, and today both sides of the family should be treated more equally.

A better approach is to focus on company and storytelling, with wine as an accompaniment; offer what the elder actually likes and can safely drink; and spend the budget on travel, a health check or a family gathering rather than on table count and bottle price.

Common misreadings

First, treating birthday wine as a brand or a single type. It is a use name, not a category.

Second, assuming every round birthday must be large. “Celebrate nine, not ten” is common in many places, and rules vary by family.

Third, assuming the birthday person must drain the cup. Tea is acceptable, and younger relatives should not pressure.

Fourth, assuming baijiu is required. Yellow wine, rice wine, fruit wine and alcohol-free drinks all work.

Fifth, taking the Book of Songs blessing as evidence of a pre-Qin birthday banquet. The poem is a wish; the banquet system took shape later.

Sixth, assuming peach buns are real peaches. Traditional ones are dough figures, more symbol than food.
