Sacrificial wine

Sacrificial wine is offered to ancestors, spirits and sages, with stress on purity, vessels and order. This entry compares household, grave and public sacrifices, explains the three offerings and libation, and discusses modern disputes and the double meaning of the term.

Sacrificial wine is offered to ancestors, spirits and sages. It differs from everyday drinking not in type but in purpose and rules: the wine must be clean, vessels must match the rite, offerings must follow an order, and gestures must express reverence. Understanding sacrificial wine means understanding how wine connects people with an unseen world.

The word itself needs care. As a common noun, “sacrificial wine” is the wine used in sacrifice. As a verb, it means to make an offering with wine. As an official title, the same two characters in Chinese also denoted a senior academic or departmental post, such as the head of the Imperial College. The three senses look alike but are different, and context decides which is meant.

Time and place

There are many occasions. The Rites of Zhou pairs the four seasonal sacrifices with spring, summer, autumn and winter; later households concentrated on Qingming, the Ghost Festival, the winter solstice, New Year and death anniversaries. Regions differ in emphasis: the south stresses Qingming and the Ghost Festival, while parts of the north stress the winter solstice and New Year. Clan areas such as Huizhou, Chaoshan and southern Fujian keep spring and winter ancestral hall rites, while city families often reduce everything to an anniversary offering at home.

Public sacrifices centre on historical figures and major events, with dates set by authorities or organisations. The Qingming ceremony at the Yellow Emperor's mausoleum in Huangling, Shaanxi, and the Confucius ceremony in Qufu, Shandong, held on 28 September each year, are well-known examples. Scale, offerings, paper money, vessels and participants all vary, so one locality's practice cannot be the “standard rite”.

Sequence and vessels

A household sacrifice usually runs: cleaning, laying offerings, lighting incense, the first offering, the second offering, the third offering, reading a memorial, pouring wine on the ground, sending off the spirits and removing offerings. A grave sacrifice includes weeding, adding soil, laying offerings, presenting flowers, pouring wine, bowing and burning paper money. Public sacrifices typically include wreaths, a memorial text, silence, a wine offering and music or dance.

The three offerings are an old framework: the host offers first, the spouse or an assistant offers second, and a guest or clan member offers third. The Yili's mourning and sacrificial chapters build on this sequence. Wine has a fixed place: usually poured after incense and before bows, or poured on the ground after bows. The pouring should be steady and the vessels clean; libation offers the wine to the earth and the dead. Su Shi's line about pouring a cup to the river and moon is literary, but it shows that libation was already a familiar memorial gesture in the Song.

Vessels matter. A careful family keeps a set of ritual vessels, with jug, cup and tray not mixed with daily tableware; an ordinary family can use clean small porcelain cups. The ancient idea of “dark wine”, plain water offered alongside wine, shows that sacrifice valued returning to the origin rather than expense. The rank and combination of vessels made ritual order visible.

Symbolic meaning

First, communication. Wine is treated as a medium that can carry reverence; pouring and libation are acts of “bringing the words”. Second, purity. Sacrificial wine must be clean and free of impurities, and dedicated vessels fix that purity in practice. Third, order. The sequence of offerings expresses seniority and closeness, with principal and assistant officiants and clan members in their places. Fourth, memory. Sacrificial wine fixes family history and personal grief in a repeatable rite.

The Book of Songs repeatedly uses terms such as chang and li in sacrificial poems. Chang was a fragrant wine made from black millet and tulip-like herbs, thought to invite the spirits; li was a sweeter, lighter wine. Wine here was not only an offering but a medium of scent and communication. Later folk practice reduced this layer to “offer a cup”; the symbolism survives, but most of the specialised knowledge does not.

There is no uniform rule on type. Yellow wine, mild and clear, is common for household rites in Jiangsu and Zhejiang; baijiu is used for libation in parts of the north and southwest, usually in small quantities; rice wine and tea also appear. What matters is not price but that the wine is prepared for the rite.

Historical change

Pre-Qin ritual books set detailed rules. The Liji stresses reverence and purity, the Yili records the order of offerings, and the Book of Songs preserves the names chang and li. Wine was both offering and medium for inviting spirits. The Zhou system also managed wine through the “five qi and three wines”, distributing it by clarity and rank.

From the Han and Tang onward, state sacrifices were handled by offices such as the Court of Imperial Sacrifices, while ancestral rites depended on clans and halls. In the Song, Zhu Xi's Family Rituals designed a workable four-season ancestral rite for ordinary families, and its influence lasted into the Ming and Qing. In the Ming and Qing, clan institutions, ancestral halls, clan land and genealogies supported one another, and sacrificial wine became part of clan order.

In modern times, urbanisation and smaller households reduced large hall rites, while public sacrifices were rebuilt as ceremonies of cultural identity. In 2006 Qingming was listed in the first national intangible cultural heritage catalogue, and in 2008 it became a public holiday. The household and grave rites seen today are the result of long evolution and should not be equated directly with the pre-Qin texts.

Contemporary disputes

First, waste and environment. Large libations are seen as wasteful and may affect soil and plants; small, symbolic offerings are better. Second, safety. Incense and paper burning in cemeteries carry fire risk, and glass bottles or vessels can injure people. Third, formalism. Turning sacrifice into performance or competition over offerings weakens remembrance. Fourth, commercialisation. Paid grave visits and expensive offerings turn the rite into a service, and sincerity becomes hard to measure.

Fifth, religious boundaries. Different faiths have different rules on wine, and differences should be respected rather than forced into one standard. Sixth, drink-driving. Ancestor rites and family meals cluster around the same days, and any “just a little” excuse is unsafe. The “moderation” stressed in wine and ritual is closer to the spirit of the rite than “more wine”.

For ordinary families, the safest approach is a small amount of wine, clean vessels, quiet bowing and telling the family story. The value of the rite lies in remembering, not in display.

Common misreadings

First, treating “sacrificial wine” as only a kind of wine. It can also be an action or an official title, and context decides.

Second, assuming white spirits are required. Yellow wine, rice wine and tea are all common, and the choice follows local and family custom.

Third, equating expense with sincerity. Ritual books value purity, correct vessels and order, not price.

Fourth, assuming the wine must be shared and drunk after the rite. Some families drink it for blessing, others pour it away or leave it at the grave; both are common.

Fifth, dismissing public sacrifice as superstition. It also carries historical commemoration, local identity and tourism, and cannot be judged only by belief.

Sixth, confusing libation with urging a drink. Libation is an offering to the dead and the earth, not an excuse to make the living drink more.
