Po Zhen Zi: Xin Qiji Trims the Lamp and Looks at His Sword

Po Zhen Zi begins with “drunk, I trim the lamp and look at my sword”, moves into a dream of linked camps, a reviewed army and fast horses, keeps nine lines heroic, and then breaks the dream with “pitiful—white hair has grown”. Wine is the entrance to the dream and the night-time gesture of an ambition that cannot be fulfilled.

This song has only ten lines, and the first nine describe an army: horns through linked camps, roast beef shared among troops, border music, an autumn review, fast horses and bowstrings like thunder. The last line suddenly returns to reality: pitiful—white hair has grown! All the grandeur before it is a dream, and outside the dream the poet is old. Wine is the entrance to that dream.

The song and Chen Liang

Po Zhen Zi is the tune name, and the title is For Chen Tongfu, Written in Heroic Words and Sent to Him. Chen Tongfu is Chen Liang, a Southern Song thinker and songwriter who shared Xin Qiji's commitment to resisting the Jin. The two met at Ehu, an encounter later known as the Ehu meeting; this song was probably written while Xin Qiji was living in retirement at Daihu, though the exact year is disputed.

Xin Qiji (1140–1207) was born in Jinan in Shandong, joined the anti-Jin militia in his youth, and after returning to the south was long kept from real power, dismissed and retired several times. His heroic words are not armchair talk; he writes his actual military experience and unfulfilled ambition into the song. Heroic words in the title also appears as heroic language, with much the same meaning: powerful language sent to a friend who was equally unwilling to give up.

Text

Drunk, I trim the lamp and look at my sword; in a dream I return to the horns sounding through linked camps. Eight hundred li: roast beef shared among the troops; fifty strings: border songs played. On the autumn battlefield, the army is reviewed. Horses fly like the Dalu; bows crack like thunder. To finish the king's business under heaven, to win a name in life and after death. Pitiful—white hair has grown!

This is the received text. Old commentaries mostly take eight hundred li as the name of an ox, here meaning roast beef; the troops are the men under his command; roast means roasted meat; fifty strings refers to the se, and by extension to military instruments; play means perform; the Dalu is the swift horse traditionally ridden by Liu Bei; thunder describes the sound of the bowstring.

Drunk, trimming the lamp

“Drunk, I trim the lamp and look at my sword” is the first action of the song. Drunk shows that he has been drinking; trimming the lamp means raising the wick for light; looking at the sword means taking the sword out to look at it. The three actions together write a sleepless night: the wine has been drunk, the lamp has grown dim, and the sword is still within reach.

The key to the line is look. He is not dancing with the sword, nor polishing it, but looking at it. The action of looking has no result; it shows only one thing: this man still has war on his mind. Wine has not made him forget; it has made the thought clearer.

Dreaming of linked camps

“In a dream I return to the horns sounding through linked camps” moves the scene from the lamp to the dream. Returning in a dream means going back in a dream; horns sounding means the horn is blown; linked camps are camps joined together. The word return shows that he once lived in such camps and can now go back only in a dream.

Eight hundred li: roast beef shared among the troops; fifty strings: border songs played writes the life of the camp in the dream. Eight hundred li is the ox, sharing the roast is giving roasted beef to the troops; fifty strings are instruments, play is performance, border songs are military music. The two lines have food and sound, sharing and atmosphere, and make the army a warm whole rather than an abstract war.

“On the autumn battlefield, the army is reviewed” closes the upper half. The battlefield is the field of war, autumn is the season, reviewing the army is inspecting it. An autumn review has both a killing severity and a sense of harvest and gathering. The upper half moves from the sword under the lamp to the camp in the dream and then to the autumn parade ground, the scene widening step by step.

Horses and bows

The first two lines of the lower half write combat. “Horses fly like the Dalu” writes speed. The Dalu is the legendary swift horse said to have carried Liu Bei over the Tan River; like means resembling. The horses are as fast as the Dalu, which gives the speed of a charge. “Bows crack like thunder” writes the sound of the bow. Thunder is the thunderclap, cracking is the vibration of the bowstring; once the string sounds, it is like thunder.

These two lines do not describe the enemy or the outcome, only speed and sound. The tension of war comes from horses and bows, not from blood. Xin Qiji had fought, and he knew that what one remembers first in battle are these bodily details.

To finish the king's business under heaven, to win a name in life and after death writes ambition. To finish means to complete, the business under heaven means recovering the lost territory; to win means to gain, a name in life and after death means reputation while alive and after death. The two lines put personal fame and affairs of state together, in a strong and direct tone.

Pitiful, white hair has grown

“Pitiful—white hair has grown!” is the turn of the whole song and where its weight lies. “Pitiful” means alas, to be regretted; “white hair has grown” means his hair has turned white. The more heroic the first nine lines, the colder this line: the camps, horses, bowstrings and fame are all a dream; when the dream ends, he is only a retired man with white hair.

This ending offers no explanation and no consolation. It does not say that it is all right, that there will be another chance; it simply puts the fact down: the ambition is still there, the body is old. The power of heroic ci often comes from this refusal to avoid the gap.

Common misreadings

First, taking drunk as the pleasure of a banquet. The drunkenness here is the wine of a sleepless night, not a party.

Second, taking eight hundred li as a distance of eight hundred li. Old commentaries mostly take it as the name of an ox, here meaning beef.

Third, taking fifty strings as a specific instrument with fifty strings. It stands for military instruments in general and need not be itemised.

Fourth, reading the whole song as a simple hero's anthem. The last line overturns all the heroism before it; sorrow and heroism are two sides of the same song.

Fifth, taking Xin Qiji's retirement as voluntary reclusion. His years of retirement were mostly connected with dismissal and lack of employment, and should not be used as proof of indifference to fame.

At a glance: Po Zhen Zi begins with “drunk, I trim the lamp and look at my sword”, moves into a dream of linked camps, a reviewed army and fast horses, keeps nine lines heroic, and then breaks the dream with “pitiful—white hair has grown”. Wine is the entrance to the dream and the night-time gesture of an ambition that cannot be fulfilled. That is how a practical judgment about Po Zhen Zi is built.
