Shui Diao Ge Tou: Su Shi Asks the Moon with a Cup of Wine

Su Shi wrote Shui Diao Ge Tou at the Mid-Autumn festival of 1076. The preface says he drank until dawn, was very drunk, and also missed his brother Ziyou. The song moves from raising a cup to ask the blue sky to moonlight on a sleepless person, and closes with the wish that we may live long and share the same moon a thousand miles apart.

Many songs are written for the Mid-Autumn festival, and this one by Su Shi is the most often remembered. It begins with a cup of wine: not a toast at a banquet, but a single person raising a cup to question the sky. The question is not whether there is enough wine, but when the moon began to exist and what year it is tonight in heaven. Wine here is the posture of a question and the reason a loneliness can be spoken aloud.

Preface and background

The song has a short preface: In the Mid-Autumn of the bingchen year, drinking happily until dawn, very drunk, I wrote this piece, and also thought of Ziyou. Bingchen is 1076, the ninth year of the Xining era. Su Shi was then at Mizhou, whose seat is today's Zhucheng in Shandong; Ziyou is his younger brother Su Zhe. Su Shi was born in 1037 and died in 1101, so he was thirty-nine that year.

Drinking happily until dawn shows a night of wine that lasted until morning; very drunk shows that he did not hide his drunkenness; and also thought of Ziyou states the occasion plainly: after the festivity, he remembered a brother he had not seen for years. The preface is not decoration. It sets two tones for the whole song: the release of wine on one side, and the absence of a person on the other.

Text

When will the bright moon appear? I raise my cup and ask the blue sky. I do not know what year it is tonight in the palaces of heaven. I wish to ride the wind home, yet I fear the jade towers and pearl halls, where the heights are too cold to bear. I rise and dance, playing with my clear shadow; how does that compare with being among men?

The moon turns past the red pavilion, sinks low at the carved window, and shines on one who cannot sleep. It should bear no resentment; why must it be full when people are apart? People have grief and joy, parting and meeting; the moon has its dark and bright, waxing and waning; this has never been whole since ancient times. I only wish that we may live long, and share this same moonlight a thousand miles apart.

This is the received text. “How does it compare with the human world” also appears as “when is it in the human world,” and “and yet I fear” also appears as “only I fear”; the variants do not change the overall tone, though they make the choice between staying below and returning above seem more hesitant or more decided.

The upper half: asking the sky with wine

The opening two lines join an action to a question. “When will the bright moon appear” is a question that cannot be answered, and raising a cup to ask the blue sky turns that unanswerable question into a posture. Li Bai wrote, “When did the moon first come to the blue sky? Tonight I stop my cup and ask it”; Su Shi's phrasing and situation clearly continue from Li Bai. The difference is that Li Bai stops his cup while Su Shi raises his, and the question is still the same heaven that gives no answer.

“I do not know what year it is tonight in the palaces of heaven” brings time into the question as well. Men count years and months; does heaven? The question looks naive, but it turns this Mid-Autumn moment into an uncertain hour. “I wish to ride the wind home” proposes the impulse to leave, while “yet I fear the jade towers and pearl halls, where the heights are too cold to bear” immediately supplies the hesitation: heaven may be beautiful, but it may be too cold. The cold is both temperature and solitude.

“I rise and dance, playing with my clear shadow; how does that compare with being among men?” is the first landing of the whole song. Rather than freeze in imagination, it is better to dance below with one's own shadow. The image is close to Li Bai's Drinking Alone under the Moon: both are a person keeping company with a shadow under the moon. But Su Shi does not stop at solitude; with “how does that compare with being among men” he places the choice firmly back in the human world.

The lower half: moonlight on the sleepless

The lower half shifts to the moon's point of view. “The moon turns past the red pavilion, sinks low at the carved window, and shines on one who cannot sleep” describes the moon's movement: past the red tower, low into the latticed window, onto a person who cannot sleep. The moon has no feelings, yet it is fullest exactly when people are not together, and so comes the complaint: “It should bear no resentment; why must it be full when people are apart?” The line asks the moon on behalf of a person, and asks fate on behalf of himself.

Then the poet answers half of the question himself. People have grief and joy, parting and meeting; the moon has its dark and bright, waxing and waning; this has never been whole since ancient times. He places human partings and meetings under the same law as the moon's phases: incompleteness is not an accident but the norm. This is not a stock consolation, because it first admits that this has never been whole, and only then offers a wish.

“I only wish that we may live long, and share this same moonlight a thousand miles apart” closes the song. The moon stands for what is shared; even a thousand miles apart, two people can share the same light. Wine is in the preface, the moon is in the whole song; wine brings the release of this one night, the moon leaves a longer connection.

The place of wine and moon in this song

First, wine is the entrance. Without raising a cup, the song would not begin in the posture of questioning the sky; wine gives a loneliness on a Mid-Autumn night an action.

Second, wine is the background, not the theme. What recurs in the song is the moon: asking it, returning to it, resenting it, sharing it. Wine appears only at the beginning and in the preface, then withdraws. This is the opposite of Bring in the Wine, where wine stands at the centre of every line; here wine opens the song and the moon carries the feeling.

Third, wine is the bridge between drinking happily and being sleepless. The preface says he drank until dawn, the lower half says the moon shines on one who cannot sleep; festivity and insomnia are not contradictory, they are two sides of the same night.

It should be said that Song literati mostly drank low-strength grain wines such as yellow wine and rice wine; the craft lineage of yellow wine is a useful reference, but very drunk cannot be converted directly into today's strong baijiu. Drunkenness here is a state of feeling, not an alcohol percentage.

Textual variants and reading

This song circulated very widely, and its variants and punctuation differ too. Common differences include “how does it compare” versus “when is it,” and “and yet I fear” versus “only I fear”; different anthologies also punctuate “I rise and dance, playing with my clear shadow; how does that compare with being among men?” slightly differently. When reading, grasp the structure first: the upper half asks the sky, the lower half asks the moon; the upper half wants to return, the lower half accepts that perfection is impossible. Once the structure is clear, small differences in wording do not disturb the meaning.

There is also a common misreading: taking “I only wish that we may live long” as a wish only about lovers. It was first written to a brother and points to family affection; it later came to be used widely for all kinds of parting and longing, a natural extension in reception history, but that does not mean it should be restricted to a single object.

Common misreadings

First, taking the palaces of heaven as a specific palace. They are an imagined moon palace, not a geographical building.

Second, taking “the heights are too cold to bear” as temperature alone. It also writes the cold solitude of a high political or personal position, though not every line needs to be forced into that reading.

Third, taking “it should bear no resentment” as meaning the moon really has no resentment. The poet asks on the moon's behalf, then answers for himself.

Fourth, taking the moon as a beautiful woman. In this song it stands for the moon itself.

Fifth, reading the song as pure optimism. Its ground is parting and incompleteness; the wish moves precisely because it admits that completeness is impossible.

Short definition: Su Shi wrote Shui Diao Ge Tou at the Mid-Autumn festival of 1076. The preface says he drank until dawn, was very drunk, and also missed his brother Ziyou. The song moves from raising a cup to ask the blue sky to moonlight on a sleepless person, and closes with the wish that we may live long and share the same moon a thousand miles apart.
