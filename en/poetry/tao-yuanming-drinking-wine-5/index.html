Drinking Wine, Number Five: Tao Yuanming Sees the Southern Mountain

Number Five of Drinking Wine describes building a hut among men without the noise of carriages and horses, picking chrysanthemums by the eastern fence and seeing the southern mountain, and ending on “there is a true meaning in this, but I have forgotten the words to explain it”. The poem itself never uses the word wine; wine belongs to the title and the preface of the sequence, and must be read within Tao Yuanming's return to farm life.

This poem is so famous that it is easily taken for a still picture of farm life: a man lives among people yet hears no carriages or horses; he picks chrysanthemums by the fence, looks up and sees the southern mountain; the mountain air is fine, the birds return, and there is a meaning in it that cannot be put into words. But reading only this poem misses a fact: it belongs to a sequence of twenty Drinking Wine poems, and the poem itself never uses the word wine.

The sequence and wine

Drinking Wine is a sequence of twenty poems. The general preface says that the poet lived in idleness with little joy, that the autumn nights were growing long, that he happened to have good wine and drank every night without fail, and that after getting drunk he wrote a few lines for his own amusement. The preface states the setting clearly: wine is the occasion for writing the sequence, not a prop that every poem must name.

Number Five is often singled out because it turns the state after drinking into looking at a mountain, at birds and at his own mind, rather than into drunkenness or capacity. Wine withdraws into the title and the preface, and what remains in the poem is an evening so clear it is almost transparent. This way of containing wine without writing it is what separates the poem from poems that urge others to drink.

Tao Yuanming (about 365–427) was an Eastern Jin poet who held office several times before returning to farm life. His drinking is not banquet courtesy but part of that return: when there is wine he drinks, when drunk he writes, and the next day he still farms, reads and watches the clouds. Wine here is best understood as part of that life, not an isolated literary symbol.

Text

I built my hut among men, yet hear no noise of carriages and horses. You ask how this can be? A heart far away makes the place itself remote. Picking chrysanthemums by the eastern fence, I see the southern mountain at ease. The mountain air is fine at evening; birds return together. There is a true meaning in this; I want to explain it, but have forgotten the words.

This is the received text. Building a hut means making a dwelling, among men means where people gather, and the noise of carriages and horses means the clamour of traffic and also of social calls and rushing about. Seeing also appears as gazing; there is a true meaning in this also appears as here there is still a true meaning, and explain also appears as distinguish.

The first four lines: distance within the human world

The opening two lines give a seeming contradiction: a house built in the human world, yet without the noise of carriages and horses. The next two immediately ask and answer: how is this possible? Because the heart is far, the place becomes remote of itself.

The point of these four lines is not withdrawal but distance. The poet has not moved into deep mountains; he still lives among people. What changes is not his geographical position but his relation to noise. A heart far away is not indifference; it is the ability not to be led away by things. The place becoming remote is not a geographical judgement but the quiet projected by a state of mind.

To read a heart far away as escaping reality narrows the poem. After returning to the farm, Tao Yuanming still had to farm, pay rent and deal with neighbours; he did not leave the human world, he simply stopped treating the direction of carriages and horses as his own.

Picking chrysanthemums and the southern mountain

“Picking chrysanthemums by the eastern fence, I see the southern mountain at ease” contains the two most famous lines. The first is an action: picking chrysanthemums by the eastern fence; the second is a line of sight: seeing the southern mountain without intending to. At ease describes the rhythm of the action, not a deliberate pose; seeing describes the natural arrival of the gaze, not a search upward.

These lines move because there is no causal relation between the action and the scenery. The poet did not go to pick chrysanthemums because he wanted to see the mountain, nor see the mountain because he picked chrysanthemums; the two happen naturally in the same evening. The chrysanthemum is at hand, the mountain is far; one is in the hand, one at the horizon. The poem places near and far in one sentence without making them explain each other.

Later readers often connect picking chrysanthemums with the Double Ninth and chrysanthemum wine. That connection is real in cultural history, but the poem itself writes neither wine nor a festival; it writes only picking chrysanthemums and seeing the mountain. Inserting later customs back into the poem adds things that are not there.

Mountain air, returning birds and true meaning

“The mountain air is fine at evening” writes the colour of the mountain at dusk. Mountain air is the mist and breath of the mountain, evening is the time when the sun slopes west; the two characters fix the hour and make the air visible. Birds return together writes birds going home in company; together means in a flock, and the direction of their return quietly answers the direction in which the poet stands.

The last two lines close the poem. There is a true meaning in this says that the scene before him holds a real significance; I want to explain it, but have forgotten the words says that he wants to distinguish and speak it clearly, yet has already lost the language. This is not mystification but an admission that some experience cannot be fully translated into concepts. Tao Yuanming does not tell the reader what the true meaning is; he only lets the reader see what he saw.

Variants and seeing versus gazing

The variants here are worth noticing. The Wenxuan text reads gazing at the southern mountain at ease, and here there is still a true meaning, I want to distinguish it but have forgotten the words; the received text mostly reads seeing the southern mountain, there is a true meaning in this, and I want to explain it.

The difference between seeing and gazing is subtle: seeing is to catch sight of without intending, gazing is to look into the distance on purpose. Su Shi praised the character for seeing, thinking it better captured the moment when scene and mind meet; that is later criticism, not the only answer. A reader can try both versions and feel how the active and passive directions of the gaze change the breathing of the whole poem.

Common misreadings

First, thinking the poet really lived cut off from the world. He lived among men; only his heart kept its distance from carriages and horses.

Second, taking a heart far away as passive withdrawal. It is more like an actively chosen measure of life.

Third, equating picking chrysanthemums directly with the Double Ninth or chrysanthemum wine. The poem has no festival and no wine; those are later cultural associations.

Fourth, taking I have forgotten the words as a word game. It is an honest admission of the limits of language.

Fifth, reading this as a drinking poem and looking for capacity. The poem has no word for wine; wine is in the sequence preface and the title.
