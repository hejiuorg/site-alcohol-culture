Seeing Off Yuan the Second on His Mission to Anxi: One Cup Becomes a Song

Wang Wei's “Seeing Off Yuan the Second on His Mission to Anxi” opens with morning rain at Weicheng and green willows by the guest house, then lands on “one more cup before you go: west of Yang Pass there are no old friends”. It compresses a farewell into a cup, and was already sung in the Tang before becoming the “Three Refrains on Yang Pass”.

There are many farewell poems, but few have been set to music and sung for a thousand years. Wang Wei's poem has only four lines, yet it writes a long journey completely: rain, willows, wine, Yang Pass. It does not weep and does not declaim; it simply urges one more cup, because once past Yang Pass there will be no old friends.

Full text

Morning rain at Weicheng dampens the light dust; green willows stand fresh by the guest house. I urge you to finish one more cup: west of Yang Pass there are no old friends.

In romanisation:

Wei cheng zhao yu yi qing chen, ke she qing qing liu se xin. Quan jun geng jin yi bei jiu, xi chu Yang guan wu gu ren.

The poem has twenty-eight characters in four seven-character lines and is a regulated quatrain. It is also known as “Weicheng Qu” and, from its last line, as “Yang Pass Song”.

Line-by-line notes

Weicheng: the old city of Xianyang, capital of Qin, renamed Weicheng in the Han and located north-east of present-day Xianyang in Shaanxi, on the north bank of the Wei River. It was the necessary departure point for the westward road from Chang'an. Zhao yu: morning rain. Yi: to moisten, to dampen. Qing chen: light dust on the road. The rain is not heavy; it settles the dust without turning the road to mud, which is exactly what a traveller needs.

Ke she: a guest house or post station, where the farewell banquet is held. Qing qing: a deep, fresh green. Liu se xin: the willow colour is new after rain. Willow is a common farewell image because its sound suggests “to stay”, and breaking a willow branch was a Tang custom of parting; the line is both scenery and a hint of holding back.

Quan jun: I urge you. Geng jin: to drain once more. Yi bei jiu: one cup of farewell wine. Xi chu: to go west out of the pass. Yang Guan: a frontier gate established in the Western Han, south-west of present-day Dunhuang in Gansu, where the road split north to Yumen Pass and south through Yang Pass, the gateway from the Central Plains to the Western Regions. Gu ren: old friends, people one knows.

The wine image

The cup does not quench thirst; it delays. The poet knows he cannot keep the traveller, so he puts the hope of staying into the cup: finish this one, and then it really is time to go. The action is small, yet heavier than a wish for a safe journey, because it requires both people to carry it out, one urging and one drinking.

“West of Yang Pass there are no old friends” joins geography to feeling. Beyond the pass, post stations, acquaintances and the sound of home fall away, and wine becomes the last stretch of time in which old friends still exist. The more ordinary the wine, the heavier the line: it relies on no famous vintage and no feast, only on the act of drinking together.

Wine also marks a boundary. Inside the pass is a society of acquaintances; outside are unfamiliar garrison towns and trade routes, and one cup separates the two.

Tang farewells were usually held at post pavilions, with wine at the centre of the ritual. Breaking willow, urging wine and writing poems often came in sequence: the willow for holding back, the wine for seeing off, the poem for recording. Wang Wei compresses the three into four lines, scenery and willow first, wine and person after, an order that matches a real farewell banquet.

Background

Wang Wei (701–761), courtesy name Mojie, was born in Puzhou to a family registered in Qi County, Taiyuan. He passed the metropolitan examination in 721 and rose to the post of vice minister of the right, hence the name “Wang Youcheng”. He was a master of poetry, painting and music, and in later life retired to Wangchuan in Lantian, writing landscape and rural poetry.

The poem was probably written in the Tianbao period, while Wang Wei held office in Chang'an. His friend “Yuan the Second” bore the surname Yuan and was second in his family; his personal name is unknown. He was ordered on a mission to Anxi. The Anxi Protectorate was established at Xizhou in 640 and moved to Kucha, in present-day Xinjiang, in 658; it was the Tang military and administrative office governing the Western Regions.

From Chang'an to Anxi one travelled the Hexi Corridor and left through Yang Pass or Yumen Pass, a journey of thousands of li. Friends could escort a traveller only as far as Weicheng, and the rest of the road had to be travelled alone. “No old friends” is therefore not a figure of speech but a practical judgement about the journey.

Wang Wei knew the route himself. In 737 he travelled to the Hexi frontier as an investigating censor, and he had direct experience of the distance and the strangeness of garrison towns.

Textual variants

The first line is uniform in the sources. The second line exists as “green willows, new colour” and as “green spring willows”, the first describing colour after rain and the second naming the season; the standard text usually follows the first. In the third line, “finish” is sometimes written with a character meaning “advance”: “finish” stresses draining the cup, “advance” stresses offering another, a slight difference in tone.

The title appears in the Complete Tang Poems as “Seeing Off Yuan the Second on His Mission to Anxi”, while commentators also give “Weicheng Qu” and “Yang Pass Song”. Citations should name the edition rather than mixing variants.

Later influence

The poem was already sung in the Tang. Bai Juyi (772–846), in one of his poems on drinking, writes of “listening to the fourth refrain of Yang Pass”, which shows that by the mid-Tang it was a standard farewell song at banquets, and that its performance was divided into sections.

From the Song onward it was repeatedly set for the qin and became known as “Three Refrains on Yang Pass”. “Three refrains” usually means that certain lines are sung repeatedly, or that the piece falls into three sections; the exact manner differs between scores and should not be reduced to one authorised version.

Its influence also entered the language. Yang Pass became a byname for parting, and “west of Yang Pass there are no old friends” has been quoted and adapted ever since. A poem of twenty-eight characters could enter poetry, music, painting and everyday speech at once, precisely because of that cup that is never quite finished.
