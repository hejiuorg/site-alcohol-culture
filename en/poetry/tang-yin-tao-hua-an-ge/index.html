Song of the Peach Blossom Hut: Tang Yin Pays for Wine with Peach Blossoms

The Song of the Peach Blossom Hut describes a “peach blossom immortal” living in a hut among peach trees: he plants the trees, picks the blossoms and trades them for wine money, sits before the flowers when sober and sleeps beneath them when drunk, and would rather die old among flowers and wine than bow before carriages and horses. It makes honest poverty sound exuberant, then asks what remains of the tombs of the great. The poem survives in widely differing versions, so the text used must be stated.

The poem survives in widely differing versions: “picks peach blossoms to trade for wine money” appears in some texts as “breaks a flowering branch to pay for wine”; “comes back to sleep beneath the flowers” appears as “must sleep beneath the flowers”; “half drunk, half awake” appears as “half awake, half drunk” or as “before and after the flowers”; “the rich” appears as “the noble”.

The poem opens like a fairy tale: in the Peach Blossom Dell there is a Peach Blossom Hut, in the hut lives a peach blossom immortal, the immortal plants peach trees and picks the blossoms to trade for wine money. It is not a quiet pastoral but a self-portrait with a performance in it: the poet writes himself as a peach blossom immortal, turns honest poverty into a choice, and makes drinking a daily act. At the end the tone shifts, and he asks through the tombs of the great what wealth can leave behind.

The Peach Blossom Dell and the Peach Blossom Hut

Tang Yin (1470–1524), courtesy name Bohu and also Ziwu, called himself Liuru Jushi and Master of the Peach Blossom Hut; he came from Wu County, present-day Suzhou in Jiangsu. In the eleventh year of the Hongzhi reign (1498) he took the provincial examination at Yingtian and came first, and was known as Tang the Jieyuan; the next year (1499) he went to the capital for the metropolitan examination and was implicated in an examination scandal, which ended his official career. He then returned to Suzhou and lived by poetry, calligraphy and painting.

The Peach Blossom Dell lay north of Suzhou, and Tang Yin built the Peach Blossom Hut there and called himself its master. The exact year of the building and of this poem is disputed; it is generally placed after his return to Wu, around the Zhengde reign. The place matters: it is not an imagined paradise but a property in Suzhou, and the peach blossom immortal in the poem is the name the man living there gave himself.

Text

In the Peach Blossom Dell there is a Peach Blossom Hut; beneath the hut, a peach blossom immortal. The peach blossom immortal plants peach trees, and picks peach blossoms to trade for wine money. When sober he only sits before the flowers; when drunk he comes back to sleep beneath them. Half drunk, half awake, day after day; flowers fall, flowers open, year after year. I would rather grow old and die among flowers and wine than bow before carriages and horses. Dust of carriages and hooves is the pleasure of the rich; a wine cup and flowering branches are the lot of the poor. If you compare wealth with poverty, one is on the ground and one in the sky. If you compare poverty with carriages and horses, they get to gallop and I get my leisure. Others laugh at me for being too crazy; I laugh at them for not seeing through. Do you not see the tombs of the great at Wuling: no flowers, no wine, hoed into fields.

This poem entry follows one received text, the one given in the Palace Museum publication. The poem has many variants, set out below under Textual variants. The Peach Blossom Dell is a place name in Suzhou; the Peach Blossom Hut is the property Tang Yin built; the peach blossom immortal is the poet's name for himself; trading for wine money means the wine had to be paid for with the proceeds of the blossoms; before carriages and horses means before the carriages of the powerful; dust of carriages and hooves stands for the bustle of the rich; a wine cup and flowering branches stand for the daily life of the poor; Wuling first meant the five imperial tombs of the Han and later stood for the quarters of the nobility.

Peach blossoms for wine: livelihood and drink in the poem

“And picks peach blossoms to trade for wine money” is the most concrete line in the whole poem. The lines before make the peach blossom immortal sound like a god, and this line suddenly lands on money: the wine is not given by anyone and not stored at home, but traded for with picked blossoms. The fairy quality and the livelihood sit together, and the figure becomes believable.

It also shows that the wine here is everyday and cheap, not the grape wine in a luminous cup. Ming grain wine is related in craft to today's Yellow Wine, though the specific drinks of the time cannot be equated directly with today's yellow wine. The poem gives no brand, origin or taste, only wine money; the point is a way of life in which one finds a way to pay for wine.

Trading for wine money carries another meaning: peach blossoms are ordinarily for viewing, and the poet treats them as something that can be exchanged. This is not vandalism of beauty but a joining of beauty to life: flowers can be looked at and can be traded for wine; poetry can be refined and can also count money. This mixing of the refined and the ordinary is exactly Tang Yin's specialty.

Before and beneath the flowers: the cycle of waking and drinking

The next four lines write a cycle: when sober he only sits before the flowers; when drunk he comes back to sleep beneath them; half drunk, half awake, day after day; flowers fall, flowers open, year after year. Waking and drunkenness, sitting and sleeping, days and years all appear in pairs. Time here is not a straight line but a circle that repeats.

This makes drinking a rhythm rather than an event. The poet does not write one particular banquet or one particular drunken night; he writes a state repeated every day before and beneath the flowers. Wine here is not a social tool but a way of dividing the days: sitting before the flowers when awake, sleeping beneath them when drunk, and another day passes half drunk and half awake.

Flowers fall, flowers open, year after year puts the cycle of flowers beside the cycle of a person. The flowers will open again, but a person will not return to the same year; yet the surface tone is light, as if the cycle could go on forever. Between the light tone and the irreversibility of time lies the tension of the poem.

I would rather grow old among flowers and wine: wealth against poverty

“I would rather grow old and die among flowers and wine than bow before carriages and horses” is the poem's declaration of attitude. Among flowers and wine is a life between blossoms and drink; bowing before carriages and horses is bowing to the powerful. The poet sets the two lives side by side and chooses the first without ambiguity.

Dust of carriages and hooves is the pleasure of the rich; a wine cup and flowering branches are the lot of the poor develops the contrast. The pleasure of the rich is the dust and hooves raised by their carriages; the lot of the poor is a cup and branches. These two lines are not an objective census but a judgement of value: the poet ranks leisure above galloping.

If you compare wealth with poverty, one is on the ground and one in the sky. “If you compare poverty with carriages and horses, they get to gallop and I get my leisure” makes the argument plain with two comparisons. The first says the gap between wealth and poverty is like sky and ground; the second says that if you reverse the comparison, the powerful get to rush about and I get leisure. The comparison is not meant to prove poverty better but to change the standard of what is good: not rank but freedom.

Others laugh at me: the wild man's defence

The last four lines turn to other people. “Others laugh at me for being too crazy; I laugh at them for not seeing through” is a pair of laughs: they laugh at his madness, he laughs at their blindness. This is not mere spite but a defence of himself: the things you value, I can see the end of.

Do you not see the tombs of the great at Wuling: no flowers, no wine, hoed into fields offers the evidence. Wuling was the site of the Han imperial tombs and later stood for the quarters of the nobility; the tombs of the great at Wuling are the graves of the once powerful. Those who were once eminent now have no flowers and no wine on their graves, and the ground has even been hoed into fields. Set against the life among flowers and wine, the permanence of wealth is put in question.

The ending does not say that poverty is certainly happy; it says that wealth cannot guarantee being remembered. The poet uses the grave to bring the glory before carriages and horses down to the level of a field, and then returns to his flowers and wine. The tone is wild, but the logic is cold.

Textual variants

The Song of the Peach Blossom Hut is one of the Tang Yin poems with the greatest variation in its transmission, and different anthologies differ in wording and even in the order of lines. Common differences include: picks peach blossoms to trade for wine money appears in some texts as breaks a flowering branch to pay for wine; when drunk he comes back to sleep beneath them appears as when drunk he must sleep beneath them; half drunk, half awake, day after day; flowers fall, flowers open, year after year appears as half awake, half drunk, day after day, or as before and after the flowers, day after day; wine and waking, year after year; dust of carriages and hooves is the pleasure of the rich appears with the noble for the rich; others laugh at me for being too crazy varies between too and exceedingly. These differences are not only in individual characters; they change the rhythm and tone of the lines. A quotation should therefore state the text it follows and should not splice lines from different versions. This poem entry follows the text in the Palace Museum publication and checks famous lines against Gushiwen.

Common misreadings

First, taking the peach blossom immortal as a real god. It is a name Tang Yin gave himself, behind which stands a Suzhou property.

Second, taking trading for wine money as a romantic figure of speech. It writes an actual livelihood: the wine had to be bought with the proceeds of the flowers.

Third, taking the poem as a pure declaration of pleasure. It also reorders the values of wealth and poverty.

Fourth, taking the tombs of the great at Wuling as five specific graves. Wuling is a collective name for the Han imperial tombs, used here for the end of the eminent.

Fifth, mixing lines from different versions in a quotation. The poem has many variants, and the text used should be stated.
