Drinking Alone under the Moon: Li Bai's Pot of Wine and Three Shadows

Li Bai's “Drinking Alone under the Moon” turns solitude into a little theatre with the line “I raise my cup to invite the bright moon, and with my shadow we become three”. The moon and shadow cannot drink, yet the poet recruits them as company. Wine here is not a remedy for sorrow but a prop for an imaginative stage.

Among Tang poems about drinking, few turn “drinking alone” into a short play with a set, characters and a beginning, middle and end as clearly as “Drinking Alone under the Moon”. Flowers, a pot of wine, the moon and a shadow are arranged and rearranged by the poet, until the poem settles on the line “forever to join in a journey without feeling, meeting far away in the Milky Way”. Reading it means seeing not only the dash of “raising a cup to the bright moon” but also the arrangement and self-awareness behind that dash.

What happens in the poem

The poem opens directly: “Among the flowers, a pot of wine; I drink alone, with no one near.” The setting is a bed of flowers, the wine is only one pot, and there is no one to share it. Then the poet acts: “I raise my cup to invite the bright moon; with my shadow we become three.” He lifts the cup, invites the moon, adds his own shadow, and makes a group of three.

But these “three” are not solid. The poet immediately admits: “The moon does not understand drinking; the shadow merely follows my body.” The moon cannot drink, and the shadow only moves with him. So he steps back: “For now I shall keep the moon and shadow as companions; one should enjoy life while spring is here.” Then comes singing and dancing—“I sing, and the moon lingers; I dance, and the shadow scatters”—as if the moon and shadow really responded. Finally comes a sober judgement: “When sober we share the joy; when drunk we scatter.” The poem ends by promising the brief gathering to the distant sky: “Forever to join in a journey without feeling, meeting far away in the Milky Way.”

Li Bai and the background of the poem

Li Bai (701–762) is one of the most famous poets of the High Tang. Britannica records that he arrived in Chang'an in 742 hoping for a court post, received no official position but was accepted into a group of distinguished court poets, and began wandering again in autumn 744. This experience is often used to explain the solitude and self-assertion in “Drinking Alone under the Moon”.

Yet it is not safe to tie the poem to a single year or event. Its exact date is unsettled; “the disappointed years in Chang'an” is a common reading background, not a provable fact. A more reliable statement is that the solitude in the poem is connected to Li Bai's long wandering, his search for office and his self-image, but the poem should not be reduced to a diary entry for one night.

What kind of wine is in the pot

A “pot” is a wine vessel, and “one pot” suggests a modest amount, enough for one person rather than the large jars of a banquet. The wine commonly drunk in the Tang was a grain-fermented wine, lower in alcohol than today's distilled spirits, and it was often warmed before drinking. It has a craft kinship with today's yellow wine, but the two cannot be simply equated; see Yellow Wine for the grain-wine lineage.

The poem does not specify the type, origin or age of the wine. That blank matters: the poet cares not about quality but about the action the wine makes possible—raising the cup, inviting the moon, facing the shadow. Wine here is closer to a prop that helps him build an imagined space to inhabit for a while.

Three figures: the moon, the shadow and the poet

“With my shadow we become three” is often taken as a romantic image, but the poem assigns the three roles clearly. The poet is the subject, the moon is the invited guest, and the shadow is an appendage cast by the body. They are not equal: the moon cannot drink, the shadow cannot speak, and only the poet can actually hold the cup.

The scene moves us precisely because it is unequal. The poet does not truly believe that the moon and shadow will drink with him; he knows they cannot. Yet he invites them anyway. This is a deliberate fiction: knowing the other cannot respond, he still performs companionship. Wine gives that fiction a concrete action; without it, solitude would remain an inner state, and only after raising the cup does it become something visible.

The structure of drunk and sober

“When sober we share the joy; when drunk we scatter” is the key to the whole poem. We usually assume that drinking makes a gathering livelier, but the poet says that when sober the three can still be together, and when drunk they scatter. “Sober” and “drunk” here are not only physical states but two kinds of relationship: when sober, the poet can still sustain the imagination; when drunk, even the imagination cannot hold.

The next line, “forever to join in a journey without feeling”, turns the poem upward. “Without feeling” is easily read as “heartless”, but in classical poetry it more often means “without the entanglements of worldly emotion” or “the moon and shadow have no feelings of their own”. Knowing that human company cannot last, the poet promises the meeting to the Milky Way and the high sky. This is not simple self-consolation but a way of raising solitude to a cosmic scale.

From “drinking alone” to a “journey without feeling”

The title says “drinking alone”, yet the poem keeps manufacturing the illusion of company: inviting the moon, facing the shadow, singing, dancing. At the end the poet gives up the illusion and entrusts companionship to the “feelingless” moon and shadow. The process has three steps: admitting solitude, inventing company, and moving beyond solitude.

Wine works in all three. Without wine, the poet would not raise the cup; without the cup, the moon and shadow would not enter the picture; without the change between drunk and sober, he would not notice how brief the company is. Wine is not a cure but a medium that externalises inner solitude into an event that can be watched and written down.

Common misreadings

First, reading the poem as a straightforward poem of bold drinking. It has a bold side, but its ground is solitude and self-arrangement, not a lively banquet.

Second, taking the “three” as three real people. The moon and shadow are roles invited by the poet, not companions in the room.

Third, understanding “without feeling” as “heartless”. In the poem it is closer to the moon and shadow having no worldly emotions, or to a companionship beyond human feeling.

Fourth, taking “one pot of wine” as heavy drinking. The size of the pot and the strength of Tang wine differ from today, and the poem's focus is not on quantity.

Fifth, fixing the background too precisely. Li Bai's disappointment in Chang'an is a common explanation, but the exact year and situation are unsettled.

How to read this drinking poem today

The poem can be read as a guide to solitude, though one need not copy the poet's method. It offers a way of handling loneliness: first admit that no one is with you, then build a scene with imagination, and finally accept that the scene will end. Wine is only the prop of that scene; replace it with tea, music or a walk and the structure still holds.

For wine's place in a longer cultural history, read Time in a Cup; for the distance between Tang grain wine and today's yellow wine, see Yellow Wine; to return to the poet himself, read Li Bai. The poem offers no drinking advice, but it offers a way of looking at solitude.
