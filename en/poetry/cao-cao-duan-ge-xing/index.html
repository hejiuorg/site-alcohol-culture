Duan Ge Xing: Cao Cao Turns Wine into the Anxiety of Seeking Talent

Duan Ge Xing is an old yuefu title traditionally assigned to Cao Cao. It begins with “I face the wine and should sing; how long is a life?”, uses wine to dissolve sorrow, moves into the anxiety of seeking talent, and closes with the Duke of Zhou spitting out his food to receive guests. Wine is not pleasure here but the medium that places a short life and an unfinished enterprise on the same table.

“I face the wine and should sing; how long is a life?” is one of the most familiar lines in Chinese poetry, so familiar that it is easily taken for a poem urging people to enjoy the moment. But reading the whole piece shows that wine appears only in the first half; the second half is entirely about seeking talent: blue collars, deer calls, a bright moon and few stars, the Duke of Zhou. Cao Cao is not saying drink up; he is saying that life is too short and the worthy have not yet come.

Title and author

Duan Ge Xing is an old yuefu title, belonging to the Xianghe songs, Pingshui mode. Short song in a yuefu title is opposed to long song; one explanation says it refers to a short breath of song, another to a short piece, and the exact music cannot be recovered today. Traditionally the poem is assigned to Cao Cao, and the Music Treatise in the Book of Song also places it under his name.

Cao Cao (155–220) was a late Han statesman, military commander and poet. He often used old yuefu titles to write about his own times, in plain language and on a large scale. The date of this poem is not recorded precisely; the common account places it in the Jian'an years, perhaps in connection with the political and military situation around the Red Cliffs. The episode in the novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms in which he recites a poem while holding his spear is fiction and cannot be used as history. The exact year and occasion are still discussed.

Text

I face the wine and should sing; how long is a life? Like the morning dew, the days already gone are many and bitter. Stirred to generosity, sorrow is hard to forget. How to dissolve sorrow? Only Dukang. Blue, blue is your collar, long, long is my heart. But for your sake, I have pondered to this day. Deer call, deer call, they graze on the wild artemisia. I have good guests; drums and pipes play. Bright, bright as the moon, when can it be picked? Sorrow rises from within and cannot be cut off. Crossing the field paths, you have come to visit me. Long separated, we talk and feast, thinking of old kindness. Moon bright, stars few, the crows fly south. They circle the tree three times: on which branch can they rest? A mountain does not tire of height, a sea does not tire of depth. The Duke of Zhou spat out his food, and the world turned to him.

This is the received text. Only Dukang also appears with a different character, long separated, we talk and feast also appears as we talk and banquet, and the variants do not affect the overall structure.

The opening: wine and a short life

The first four lines place wine beside time. “I face the wine and should sing” can be read in two ways: facing the wine, one ought to sing; or facing the wine and the song. Either way, wine is the scene at hand. “How long is a life” is not a mathematical question but an exclamation: how long can a life be? “Like the morning dew, the days already gone are many and bitter” gives the image: like dew at dawn, dried up as soon as the sun rises; too many days have gone, too few remain.

“Stirred to generosity, sorrow is hard to forget” then pushes the emotion to a pitch of fervour, but the sorrow is not resolved. “How to dissolve sorrow? Only Dukang” gives the answer: only wine. Dukang is traditionally the founder of brewing, and here stands for wine. The line is very famous and easily misread as drinking makes you forget; the next layer of the poem immediately shows that the sorrow has not disappeared, it has only changed direction.

The middle: the anxiety of seeking talent

“Blue, blue is your collar, long, long is my heart” borrows a line from the Airs of Zheng in the Book of Songs. The original poem is about longing; Cao Cao uses it for longing after worthy men, and “but for your sake, I have pondered to this day” turns the you from a lover into a talented man, while the tone remains deeply affectionate. This is a common way of using allusion in late Han writing: the words stay, the reference changes.

Deer call, deer call, they graze on the wild artemisia. “I have good guests; drums and pipes play” borrows the Lesser Odes, Deer Call. The original is a song for feasting guests, and Cao Cao uses it for his wish to receive worthy men. Wine does not appear directly, but the scene of a feast is already there: good guests, strings and pipes, and a reason to raise a cup.

Bright, bright as the moon, when can it be picked? Sorrow rises from within and cannot be cut off compares the worthy to the bright moon: bright, yet hard to pick up. Picking here means taking or plucking; the moon cannot be plucked, and talent is not easily obtained, so sorrow rises and cannot be cut off. By this point wine has left the stage, and what remains is the anxiety of seeking without finding.

The moon and the crows

“Crossing the field paths, you have come to visit me” writes worthy men travelling from afar; the paths are the raised footpaths between fields, north-south and east-west. “Long separated, we talk and feast, thinking of old kindness” writes the talk and feast after a long parting; long separated carries the sense of separation and reunion. These two lines bring the search for talent down from an abstract wish to a concrete meeting and welcome.

Moon bright, stars few, the crows fly south. “They circle the tree three times: on which branch can they rest?” is the most pictorial passage in the poem. The moon is bright, the stars few, the crows fly south, circle a tree several times and still find no branch to settle on. The crows are a metaphor: talented men in a disordered age are also looking for somewhere to rest. Three times is not an exact count but repeated circling.

This metaphor also explains the wine of the first half: if the worthy of the world are like crows with no branch to rest on, then the host's wine is not pleasure but a signal, a hand raised in welcome. Wine here is the branch that can be rested on.

The ending: the Duke of Zhou and the turning of hearts

The last four lines widen the frame. “A mountain does not tire of height, a sea does not tire of depth” says that a mountain never finds itself too high, a sea never finds itself too deep; the metaphor is that the more worthy men one gathers and the larger one's capacity, the better. “The Duke of Zhou spat out his food, and the world turned to him” uses the story of the Duke of Zhou, who several times spat out the food in his mouth while eating so as not to delay a visitor. Cao Cao compares himself to the Duke of Zhou to say that he is willing to pay a price to seek talent.

The world turned to him is a political ambition and the landing point of the poem. Wine does not let the poet escape politics; it makes the political ambition more urgent: life is short, so one cannot wait; sorrow is hard to dissolve, so one must gather people.

Common misreadings

First, taking I face the wine and should sing; how long is a life as a manifesto of pleasure. What follows is the search for talent and sorrow, not a list of pleasures.

Second, taking How to dissolve sorrow? Only Dukang as a claim that wine can solve everything. The poem explicitly says sorrow rises from within and cannot be cut off; wine does not really solve it.

Third, taking blue, blue is your collar as a love poem. It borrows a line from the Book of Songs for worthy men, and the context has changed.

Fourth, taking the crows as ordinary birds. They are a metaphor for talent in a disordered age, and only together with on which branch can they rest does the image become complete.

Fifth, taking the spear-reciting episode in Romance of the Three Kingdoms as history. It is a novelistic scene and cannot replace historical sources.

For Duan Ge Xing, begin with the poet and date. Then look at the text and translation, and finally ask what it is cited for here. The answers point to different choices, not to a single score.
