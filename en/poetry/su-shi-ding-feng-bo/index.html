Ding Feng Bo: A Cloak of Mist and Rain for a Whole Life

Su Shi's Ding Feng Bo records a rainstorm on the road to Shahu on the seventh of the third month: companions were wretched, he alone was unbothered; after the rain cleared he looked back and wrote “there is neither wind nor rain, nor clear sky”. Wine appears only once—when the chill spring wind sobers him—and turns the poem from rain toward clarity.

This lyric has a very short preface explaining its occasion: on the seventh day of the third month, he met rain on the road to Shahu; the rain gear had been taken ahead, his companions were all in a sorry state, and only he did not mind; soon the sky cleared, so he wrote this. The four characters “I alone did not mind” are the entry to the whole piece—it was not that the rain was light, but that his way of receiving it was different. Wine appears only once, and it falls exactly at the turn from rain to clearing.

The text

Preface and lyric can be read separately. The preface says his companions were wretched and he alone was unbothered; the lyric begins:

Pay no heed to the sound of rain through the forest; why not sing and whistle and walk on slowly? Bamboo staff and straw sandals lighter than a horse— who is afraid? A cloak of mist and rain for a whole life.

The second half describes the clearing:

The chill spring wind sobers me from wine; a little cold, the slanting light on the hill greets me instead.

It closes with “I turn my head to where the wind and rain were; / going back, / there is neither wind nor rain, nor clear sky.” Only short passages are quoted; the whole lyric is not reproduced. The preface differs slightly in wording and punctuation between editions; the received text is followed here.

The poet and background

Su Shi (1037–1101) was a Northern Song writer accomplished in poetry, lyric, prose, calligraphy and painting. After the “Wutai poetry case” he was banished to Huangzhou, where he wrote many famous works. This lyric is usually placed in the third month of the fifth year of Yuanfeng (1082); the preface gives the specific date. Whether it was written on the spot on the Shahu road is debated, but the preface makes the time, place and situation clear.

Shahu is near Huangzhou, not the West Lake in Hangzhou. This is often mistaken. During his exile Su Shi was not well off and travelled mostly on foot or by simple means; the bamboo staff, straw sandals and rain cloak are the gear of such everyday travel. With this in mind, “lighter than a horse” is not a boast but a man encouraging himself in the rain.

Layers of the wine image

The first layer is the timing of “sobers me from wine”. The first half describes walking in the rain; the second half suddenly says the spring wind has sobered him. This means he had drunk before the rain, and the wind is gradually clearing his head. Wine is not described directly, but it marks a bodily state: from mild drink back to clarity.

The second layer is cold. “The chill spring wind sobers me from wine; a little cold” makes the after-rain bodily sense very concrete. After waking from wine he first feels cold, and only then sees the slanting light on the hill greeting him. The contrast of cold and warmth turns a change in weather into a change in mood: it is not simply that the rain stops, but that a person in the cold sees light.

The third layer is “neither wind nor rain, nor clear sky”. This is often read as a declaration of open-heartedness, but it is first an act of looking back: looking at where the rain fell, the rain has passed, and the clear sky is also passing. Since neither rain nor clear weather is permanent, neither should be taken as a verdict of fate. The clarity after wine is exactly what supports this backward look.

The fourth layer is “for a whole life”. “A cloak of mist and rain for a whole life” is not lying flat or not caring; it gathers a lifetime of weather into one cloak. Wine does not let him escape here; it lets him bear more.

Vessels and wine

The objects named are a bamboo staff, straw sandals and a rain cloak—gear for rain and travel. No wine vessel appears. The writing is restrained: wine leaves a trace only in the two characters “sobers me”, with no cup, no pot and no quantity. The reader knows only that he had drunk and was now sober.

Song wine was mainly grain-fermented—yellow wine, rice wine and regional wines—usually lower in alcohol than today's distilled baijiu. The fact that the spring wind could sober him suggests a modest amount, perhaps a small drink before the walk or something to warm himself on the way. For the lineage of grain wine, see Yellow Wine; ancient and modern drinks cannot be directly equated.

Reception

This lyric is regarded as a representative of Su Shi's open-hearted style, often mentioned together with “Nian Nu Jiao: Remembering the Past at Red Cliff” and “Shui Diao Ge Tou: When Will the Bright Moon Appear?”. Critics often note two things: the preface uses a small everyday event to draw out an attitude to life; the ending raises a specific weather into a general condition.

Other readers value the colloquial feel of “who is afraid” and “a little cold”: it is not a sermon from a platform, but a man talking to himself in the rain. This “philosophy inside the everyday” is why Su Shi's lyrics are reread. Specific comments differ; no single one should be treated as final.

Common misreadings

First, taking “neither wind nor rain, nor clear sky” as a weather report. It describes a mood after looking back, not whether tomorrow will be fine.

Second, taking “for a whole life” as giving up. It contains endurance and self-mockery, not resignation.

Third, taking Shahu as the West Lake in Hangzhou. The preface speaks of the Shahu road near Huangzhou.

Fourth, taking “sobers me from wine” as evidence of urging drink or heavy drinking. The lyric gives no quantity, only wine being blown away.

Fifth, treating the preface as an appendage. It supplies the person, time, place and contrast (companions wretched, he alone unbothered), and is key to understanding the lyric.

How to read

Read by time: rain at departure, walking in the rain, after the rain the wine clears, a little cold, slanting light, and a final backward look. Three layers of time overlap, and the lyric begins to breathe.

Then notice the person and the actions: pay no heed, why not, who is afraid, going back. These are short phrases, but they step out an attitude one by one. “Going back” is not going home; it is closer to returning to a state not governed by the weather. For the social background of Song drinking, see China; for wine in time and season, see Time in a Cup; for the tasting idea of a lingering finish, see Finish.
