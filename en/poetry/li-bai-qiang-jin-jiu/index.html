Bring in the Wine is one of Li Bai's most often quoted drinking poems, and also one of the most often reduced to a few lines. People remember enjoy life while you can and heaven gave me talents that will be used, but they often overlook how the poem moves from a rushing river to the sorrow of all ages, with a clear turn in between. Wine here is not the goal; it is how Li Bai talks with time, disappointment and friends.

Title and reading

Bring in the Wine is an old yuefu title. The character jiang is read qiang and means please. Li Bai did not invent the title but used an old one for new words. The form is a drinking song: it has the calls of a host urging guests to drink, the names of friends, and a sudden turn from the singer to himself. Read it first as a song performed at a banquet, not as a record of what was drunk.

Text

Do you not see the waters of the Yellow River coming from heaven, rushing to the sea, never to return? Do you not see the bright mirror in the high hall grieving over white hair, black silk at dawn, snow by dusk? When life is full of joy, enjoy it to the full; do not let the golden cup face the moon empty. Heaven gave me my talents, and they will be used; a thousand pieces of gold scattered, they will come back. Cook sheep and slaughter oxen, make it a feast; we should drink three hundred cups at a sitting. Master Cen, Master Danqiu, bring on the wine, do not stop the cups. I sing a song for you; please incline your ears and listen. Bells and drums, fine food and jade are not worth prizing; I only wish to be forever drunk and never wake. Since ancient times the sages and worthies have all been lonely; only the drinkers have left a name. Prince Chen once feasted at Pingle, ten thousand coins for a measure of wine, giving free rein to jest. Why should the host say there is too little money? Just go and buy wine and pour it for you. Five-flowered horses, a thousand-gold fur coat, call the boy to take them out and exchange them for fine wine, and with you dissolve the sorrow of ten thousand ages.

This is the received text. Different editions vary in wording such as golden cup, incline your ears and never wake; see below.

Reading section by section

The first two lines are about time. The Yellow River comes down from heaven, runs to the sea and does not turn back; in the mirror of the high hall, white hair appears, black silk in the morning and snow by evening. The repeated do you not see places the scale of nature beside the scale of a person: a great river on one side, the self in a mirror on the other. Irreversible time is where the poem begins.

The third and fourth lines turn to response. “When life is full of joy, enjoy it to the full” is not a slogan of pleasure-seeking but an answer to the first four lines: since time does not turn back, raise the cup while things go well. “Heaven gave me my talents, and they will be used” pulls the tone from lament back to confidence, and “a thousand pieces of gold scattered, they will come back” places money after talent. The cooking of sheep and oxen and the three hundred cups turn the banquet into a gathering with weight.

The next section names the guests. Master Cen and Master Danqiu are friends at the table, and “bring on the wine, do not stop the cups” is the host's call. Then “I sing a song for you” turns the poem into a song, while “bells and drums, fine food and jade are not worth prizing” sets wealth against wine. Here comes the line most easily misread: “I only wish to be forever drunk and never wake.” It is not a life programme but a hard saying in disappointment; the next line, that “sages and worthies have all been lonely” pushes it onto the plane of history.

The ending uses the example of Prince Chen, Cao Zhi. “Prince Chen once feasted at Pingle, ten thousand coins for a measure of wine, giving free rein to jest” connects the present banquet to a tradition of bold drinking since the Jian'an era. Then “Why should the host say there is too little money” turns abruptly to reality: do not say the money is insufficient, go and buy wine. Five-flowered horses and a thousand-gold fur coat can all be exchanged for wine, and the poem closes on dissolving the sorrow of ten thousand ages with you. The sorrow is not forgotten; it is placed for a while inside a shared drink.

What wine does in the poem

First, wine is the counterpoint to time. Water does not return and a person's hair turns white in a night; wine cannot stop time, but it makes the present bearable. Second, wine is a social medium. Cen Xun and Yuan Danqiu are present, and urging, singing and naming are ways of placing private feeling among friends. Third, wine reverses value. Bells and drums, fine food, jade and a thousand-gold coat are all set against wine; this is not a denial of reality but a temporary change of scale. Fourth, wine is self-persuasion. Bold words and disappointment stand side by side, and the poet is urging himself while urging others.

It should be said that everyday drinking in the Tang was low-strength grain wine; the craft lineage of yellow wine is related, but golden cups and measures of wine cannot be converted directly into today's 52 percent baijiu. Three hundred cups is literary exaggeration, not a medical report.

Background and textual variants

On dating, the common account is around 752, in the Tianbao era (742–756), after Li Bai had left Chang'an and his political hopes had suffered. Master Cen and Master Danqiu are usually identified as Cen Xun and Yuan Danqiu. The exact place and year are still discussed, and should not be stated too firmly.

Dunhuang manuscript P.2567 titles the poem Xizun Kong, meaning a pity that the cup is empty; it reads bright mirror by the bedside instead of in the high hall, blue clouds instead of black silk, heaven gave us followers with fine talent instead of heaven gave me my talents, and since ancient times the sages and worthies have all died instead of have all been lonely. These variants change the tone: some are more colloquial, some more violent. They show that the poem changed in transmission, and that the received text should not be treated as the only original. Reading variants is not a curiosity hunt; it is a reminder that the stability of ancient poems is less than one imagines.

Common misreadings

First, treating three hundred cups as an exact measure of drink. Measures and cups were ancient units of different capacity in different periods, and the numbers in the poem are first of all rhetoric. Second, treating “I only wish to be forever drunk and never wake” as Li Bai's outlook. It is a hard saying at a banquet, framed by the confidence of “heaven gave me my talents” and closed by the sorrow of ten thousand ages. Third, treating “sages and worthies have all been lonely” as a law of history. It is a poet's lament, not a complete judgement on history. Fourth, imagining Tang poetry through today's baijiu. The mainstream Tang drink was low-strength grain wine, with different vessels, strength and drinking methods. Fifth, quoting only the famous lines. Taking “when life is full of joy, enjoy it to the full” by itself loses the disappointment and the turn in the second half.
