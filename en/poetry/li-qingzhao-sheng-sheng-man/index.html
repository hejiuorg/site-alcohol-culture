Sheng Sheng Man: Three Cups and Two Cups of Thin Wine against the Evening Wind

Li Qingzhao's Sheng Sheng Man opens with fourteen repeated characters, then moves through a warm-cold autumn day, three cups and two cups of thin wine, fallen flowers and fine rain on the paulownia. Wine neither warms the body nor drowns sorrow; “thin” and “could not withstand” set the whole poem's helplessness.

The opening of “Sheng Sheng Man” is famous: fourteen repeated characters, one phrase after another, like a person pacing around a room, searching and searching. Then come the weather, the wine, the wild geese, the flowers, the paulownia and the rain, ending with “how could one word, sorrow, contain it all?”. Wine takes only one line, but it is the key line: it is brought out and then denied; it can warm the hands, but it cannot withstand the wind.

The text

The whole lyric is not reproduced here; only key passages are given, with a plain rendering:

Seeking and searching, cold and clear, wretched, sorrowful, grieving and grieving. A time of sudden warmth and returning cold, hardest to keep well.

In the second half, wine and geese:

Three cups and two cups of thin wine— how could they withstand the evening wind so urgent? Wild geese pass; my heart hurts; they are old acquaintances.

The ending turns to rain and sorrow:

Paulownia, and fine rain, at dusk, drip, drop. This state of things— how could one word, sorrow, contain it?

“Sudden warmth and returning cold” is weather that turns warm and cold by turns; “keep well” is to rest and recover. “Three cups and two cups” is a very small quantity; “thin wine” means a light, low-alcohol wine, not a poor one. “Withstand” is to hold off. Variants: “evening wind” is sometimes “morning wind”; the punctuation of “guarding the window, alone, how to get through to dark” differs slightly between editions. The received text is followed here.

The poet and background

Li Qingzhao (1084–c. 1155), style name Yi'an, was a Song lyric poet. In her early years she lived comfortably and collected bronzes, stone inscriptions, calligraphy and paintings with her husband Zhao Mingcheng. After the fall of the Northern Song she moved south, her life became unsettled, her husband died, and her last years were lonely. Her lyrics are known for a fine, sharp sensitivity: the early work often treats private feeling and idle sorrow, the later work the dislocation after the fall of the state and the loss of a home.

“Sheng Sheng Man” is generally thought to have been written after the move south, though the exact date is disputed and should not be fixed to a particular year. “Seeking and searching” can mean looking for a lost object, and also looking for a former life; the geese that are “old acquaintances” pull time back to the past. Wine in such a context is not the pleasure of a banquet, but the action of a person trying to settle herself in a cold wind.

Layers of the wine image

The first layer is the smallness of “three cups and two cups”. The quantity is specific but not heroic; it is a little wine poured at hand, not enough to intoxicate, not enough to warm. The first action of the lyric is “seeking”, the second “drinking”; both fail, and so comes “how could they withstand”.

The second layer is “thin”. “Thin wine” is not bad wine but wine with a light taste and low alcohol. This thinness matches the cold clarity of the whole lyric: the wine is thin, the wind urgent, the day cold, and the person in the room thinner still. Wine offers no strong stimulus; it makes the cold more clearly felt.

The third layer is “could not withstand”. Wine can warm the body and blur care, but the speaker says it cannot hold off the evening wind. This is an explicit denial: wine is not a cure. The wind is both weather and a metaphor for circumstance; wine cannot stop the wind, just as an individual cannot stop the changes of an age.

The fourth layer is the unnameable “sorrow”. The ending says “how could one word, sorrow, contain it?”, gathering all the concrete sensations into something that cannot be named. Wine along this line is a failed attempt: it tries to turn sorrow into a bearable drunkenness, and only makes the sorrow more clearly felt.

Vessels and wine

The only vessel named is the zhan, a small cup. There is no pot and no warming vessel, which suggests the wine may have been ready, at room temperature, perhaps already cold. “Three cups and two cups” is the scale of a small drink, not the large cups and pots of a banquet.

Song wine was mainly grain-fermented—yellow wine, rice wine and regional wines—usually lower in alcohol than today's distilled baijiu. “Thin wine” may mean a lighter, shorter-fermented or watered wine, or simply the speaker's sense of the wine in her mouth: when the mood is thin, the wine seems thin too. For the craft of such grain wine, see Yellow Wine; ancient and modern drinks and conditions cannot be directly equated.

Reception

What is most praised in “Sheng Sheng Man” is the opening repetition. Later critics often treat it as an invention in form: fourteen characters, layer upon layer, pressing action, setting and emotion together without seeming piled up. Others hear it as close to colloquial repetition, exactly right for a person searching and being disappointed again and again.

Critics also note how the everyday objects—wine, geese, yellow flowers, the window, paulownia, rain—are arranged into a chain of sensation from outside to inside. They often speak of its ability to make the ordinary elegant: everything named is domestic, yet the reader feels an unrelievable cold. Specific comments differ; no single one is final.

Common misreadings

First, taking “thin wine” as poor wine. It means light in taste and low in alcohol, matching the mood.

Second, taking “evening” as only “night”. It can mean toward dusk, around nightfall; the exact hour need not be fixed.

Third, reading the whole lyric only as mourning for a husband. There is grief for him, but also the dislocation after the move south and the loss of a former life.

Fourth, taking “how to get through to dark” as a question about the time of day. It describes guarding the window alone while time passes painfully.

Fifth, treating the repeated characters as wordplay. They are the rhythm of action and feeling, not a display of technique.

How to read

First read the sound. The fourteen repeated characters make the tongue and lips perform similar movements again and again, producing a feeling that cannot stop; this is the state of “seeking and searching”. Then read the temperature: sudden warmth and returning cold, the evening wind, a little cold, dusk, fine rain—the whole lyric keeps cooling.

Then read the place of wine. It appears late in the first half, between the weather and the geese; it is not a climax but a failed attempt. Read that line lightly, and it comes closer to the speaker's tone: not heroic drinking, not wild indulgence, only three cups and two cups poured out, and found useless.
