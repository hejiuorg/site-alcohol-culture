Passing an Old Friend's Farm: Meng Haoran and an Ordinary Cup of Wine

This poem describes a very ordinary visit. An old friend kills a chicken and cooks millet, and invites the poet to his home; the village is ringed with green trees, a blue mountain slopes beyond the wall; a window opens onto a threshing floor and a vegetable plot, and wine is raised while the crops are discussed; before parting they agree to meet again at the Double Ninth and admire the chrysanthemums. There is no famous wine, no rare food and no striking allusion, yet it makes going to a friend's house for a meal feel desirable.

The poem and a visit

Meng Haoran (689–740) was a High Tang poet from Xiangyang, who lived in retirement and travelled for much of his life; his poetry is known for landscape and farm life. The old friend in this poem is not identified, and the historical record gives no certain answer; from the tone, the two were close and the invitation casual, not a formal banquet.

Passing means visiting or calling at, and the old friend's farm is his country estate. The title is plain: going out to the village where an old friend lives. The poem has eight lines in five-character regulated verse and follows the whole course of the visit, from invitation to arrival, from entering the house and drinking to saying goodbye.

Text

My old friend prepares chicken and millet, and invites me to his farmhouse. Green trees ring the village; a blue mountain slopes beyond the wall. I open the window onto the threshing floor and vegetable plot; we raise our cups and talk of mulberry and hemp. When the Double Ninth comes, I shall return to the chrysanthemums.

This is the received text. Prepare means to get ready, chicken and millet means killing a chicken and cooking grain, a byword for farmhouse food; the wall is the outer city wall; the window is the opened casement; the threshing floor and vegetable plot are the working parts of a farm; mulberry and hemp stand for farming in general; and return to the chrysanthemums means to come close to them, glossed in old commentaries as admiring them.

From beyond the village to the window

The first couplet gives the invitation. “My old friend prepares chicken and millet, and invites me to his farmhouse” has no formal courtesy: the friend has prepared chicken and grain and asked him home. Chicken and millet is not a menu but a standard of hospitality, not lavish yet substantial. It also echoes the old story in the Analects of a host who killed a chicken and cooked millet for a guest, so the meal carries a plain tradition rather than a makeshift arrangement.

The second couplet gives the road and the village. “Green trees ring the village” is the near view: trees enclose the village, and the word ring gives a sense of an embrace; “a blue mountain slopes beyond the wall” is the far view: the mountain stands at a slant beyond the wall, and the slant gives the mountain a direction. One line is near, one far, one enclosing, one open, so the village is both closed and spacious.

The third couplet enters the house. “I open the window onto the threshing floor and vegetable plot” opens the casement onto the working parts of the farm; we raise our cups and talk of mulberry and hemp lifts the wine while the talk is of farming. Raising the cup means holding wine, not drinking deeply, and the topic is concrete: harvest, planting, weather. In these two lines wine has no independent status; it is simply the action that lets the conversation go on slowly.

Raising the cup and talking of farming

This line is easily read too lightly. It does not give the kind of wine, its aroma or its age, nor how much was drunk; it gives only the raising of the cup and the talk of farming happening at the same time. Wine here is social: it makes host and guest sit down, slows time, and moves the talk from courtesy to crops.

That also means the wine is everyday wine, not a collector's bottle. Ordinary people in the Tang mostly drank low-strength grain wine, and the craft lineage of yellow wine is a useful reference; wine brewed at home or bought nearby might not be clear or famous, but it was enough for a meal of chicken and millet. Imagining raising the cup as today's strong baijiu would misread the mood of the meal.

Compared with Du Fu's A Guest Arrives, both poems describe ordinary hospitality and both admit that the fare is not rich; but A Guest Arrives stresses the host's apology and urging, while this poem stresses the comfort of the setting and the ease of the conversation. Compared with Bai Juyi's Asking Liu Shijiu, that poem stops at the invitation, while this one has already brought the wine to the table.

The promise at the Double Ninth

The final couplet gives the agreement before parting. “When the Double Ninth comes, I shall return to the chrysanthemums” is not a polite formula but a fixed next meeting: on the ninth day of the ninth month I shall come back and sit near the chrysanthemums. The Double Ninth had the old customs of admiring chrysanthemums and drinking chrysanthemum wine; the poem says only return to the chrysanthemums, not drinking chrysanthemum wine, but the season and the flower are enough to suggest that cup.

This ending turns the poem from a single visit into a relationship. The poet does not say thank you for the meal, nor say that he will invite the friend next time; he says that he will come again. In farm poetry such a promise carries more weight than praise: it means neither host nor guest treats this meeting as the last.

Common misreadings

First, taking chicken and millet as literally only chicken and millet. It is a byword for farmhouse food and need not be itemised.

Second, taking raising the cup as drinking heavily. It is only holding the cup, and the poem has no drunkenness.

Third, taking mulberry and hemp as only those two crops. They stand for farming in general, the topic of conversation rather than a crop list.

Fourth, taking return to the chrysanthemums as directly drinking chrysanthemum wine. Drinking chrysanthemum wine at the Double Ninth was an old custom, but the poem writes only of coming near the flowers, not of wine.

Fifth, taking the poem as a declaration of the recluse's creed. It describes one pleasant visit, not a statement of refusal to serve; Meng Haoran's life choices were more complicated than this single poem.
