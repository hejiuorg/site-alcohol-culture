The 1855 Bordeaux Classification: A List Produced for a World's Fair

On 18 April 1855, Bordeaux brokers delivered a classification requested by the Chamber of Commerce, ranking Médoc and Graves red wines and Sauternes and Barsac sweet wines into growths. It rested on long-term market prices and reputation, not a blind tasting, and has seen only two official changes since.

On 18 April 1855, Bordeaux brokers delivered a list that sorted the region's finest red and sweet wines into ranks. The list was originally an accompaniment to the wine display at the Paris Universal Exhibition, yet it later became one of the most frequently cited quality coordinates in Bordeaux. To understand it, the key is not memorising the names of five growths but establishing who made it, why, on what basis, and what has changed since.

Before the classification: prices had already sorted the estates

Before a formal list existed, Bordeaux merchants were already ranking estates by price and reputation. The official classification body's history notes that Haut-Brion, Margaux, Latour and Lafite, because of their consistent quality and strong demand, long sold at higher prices than other estates and gradually came to be seen as a distinct “first growth” group. The 1855 list therefore did not invent the hierarchy out of nothing; it wrote down a hierarchy that already existed in the market.

This matters. The underlying logic of the classification was trade, not judging. An estate's place came from its long-term market performance and from what buyers were willing to pay, not from a prize at a single competition. When readers today encounter “first growth” or “second growth”, it is worth thinking first of price history and only then of quality assessment.

The stability of this ordering was connected to Bordeaux's commercial geography. The entry on Bordeaux shows how the city, relying on the Garonne and its port, linked inland estates to overseas markets; steady export demand allowed price records to accumulate and enabled brokers to compare estates over time.

Why it became a formal list in 1855

In 1855 Paris hosted a Universal Exhibition, and goods from across France and the world were sent to the capital. The Bordeaux Chamber of Commerce organised the local wine display, but the organisers faced a practical problem: only six bottles of each wine were to be sent to Paris, enough for display and a private tasting by judges, but not enough for ordinary visitors to taste and understand the differences among Bordeaux wines.

To make the display better convey what “the best wines of Bordeaux” meant, the Chamber decided to include a wine map of the region and to list the finest estates on it. It asked the brokers' union to supply the list. Brokers were the one party in the Bordeaux trade who knew both production and market conditions: estates knew their own wines best, merchants knew sales best, and brokers, who visited estates through the year and stood close to transactions, could combine the two kinds of information.

A list made for an exhibition thus became a classification with long-lasting authority. This origin is often overlooked: the 1855 classification was not a title that estates campaigned for, nor a public competition, but a “most reliable list” requested by the Chamber to make the exhibition display clearer.

A list completed in two weeks

On 5 April 1855 the Bordeaux Chamber of Commerce wrote to the brokers' union asking for “a list of all the red classed growths in the department, as exact and complete as possible, specifying to which of the five classes each of them belongs and in which commune they are located”. Because the Universal Exhibition was about to open, the deadline was very tight.

The brokers delivered the list on 18 April. Less than two weeks had passed. They could move so fast because they already held long-term price records, trading conventions and estate information; there was no need to stage a new competition, only to turn the existing market hierarchy into a formal text.

One detail shows the nature of the list: estates did not all have to send samples to Paris. The official history notes that beside many names on the original document the word “point”, meaning “none”, was written, indicating that no wine had been supplied for display. Inclusion therefore did not depend on what was sent that year, but on an estate's long-term trading record.

Five growths and two lists

The 1855 list actually had two parts. The red wine section was mainly Médoc, with the addition of Haut-Brion from Graves; the sweet wine section covered Sauternes and Barsac. Red wines were divided into five growths, while sweet wines had a separate structure of a superior first growth, first growths and second growths.

The official page today gives the following numbers: 61 red wine estates, with 5 first growths, 14 second growths, 14 third growths, 10 fourth growths and 18 fifth growths; and 27 sweet wine estates, with 1 superior first growth, 11 first growths and 15 second growths. These numbers are not a simple copy of the original 1855 list, but the result of estate divisions, inheritance and individual adjustments.

The list therefore has to be read on two levels: the text of 1855 and the list still in use today. Treating today's number of estates as the scale of the 19th-century list, or treating the 1855 ranks as today's quality order, both lead to error.

What the ranking was based on

The official history is clear: the classification rested on an estate's sales prices and market position over a long period, not on a single year's performance. Brokers looked at prices across a stretch of time, at an estate's stable place in trade, and at its price relationship with estates in the same and adjacent growths.

This explains why the list acquired authority so quickly. Every year, when the new vintage went on sale, merchants had to negotiate prices again; if every estate and every vintage started from zero, transaction costs would be enormous. The classification provided a common starting point: if an estate had traditionally sold at third-growth prices, buyer and seller could begin there and adjust up or down according to the vintage.

Because the basis was price and reputation, the ranking does not map neatly onto varieties or terroir. The red wines on the list are based on Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Cabernet Franc and other varieties, as discussed in Cabernet Sauvignon; an estate's position is related to its plots, soils and microclimate, as discussed in terroir. Yet these factors entered the classification through long-term market performance, not through a judge's score on the day.

Outside the list

The geographical scope of the 1855 list was limited. It covered mainly the Médoc, admitted only Haut-Brion from Graves among red wines, and concentrated its sweet wines on Sauternes and Barsac. Saint-Émilion and Pomerol were not included; both later developed their own classifications or systems of evaluation, and their absence from the 1855 list does not make them unimportant.

The list did not cover every estate either. Even within the Médoc, many unlisted estates continued to make fine wine; conversely, the performance of classed growths changed with period, management and vintage. Treating “classed growth” as a permanent guarantee of quality ignores these changes.

Lafite is one of the names most often associated with the list, as discussed in Lafite. Its position among the first growths is connected to its long price history, international demand and estate history. Understanding this is more useful than memorising the slogan of “five first growths”, because the list itself is a historical document, not a shopping guide for today.

Two official changes and the list's use today

The official history notes that since its publication the 1855 list has had only two formal changes: on 16 September 1855 Cantemerle was added to the fifth growths; in June 1973 Mouton Rothschild was raised from second to first growth. Apart from these, the ranking structure has not been comprehensively re-rated.

This does not mean the list matches reality perfectly. Estates can be divided, land can change hands, quality can rise or fall, and market prices can diverge from the official rank. The official page now presents the classification more as a promotional and reference tool for the Bordeaux region than as an annually updated quality league table. In other words, it offers a historical coordinate and a starting point for negotiation, not a legal quality certification.

Common misreadings

First, treating the 1855 classification as a blind tasting. It was not. The list rested on long-term prices and market reputation; no uniform blind tasting was organised.

Second, treating the list as permanent. It has had two official changes, and estate structures have changed through inheritance and division.

Third, treating the 1855 list as all of Bordeaux. Saint-Émilion, Pomerol and most estates in Graves were not on the original list.

Fourth, treating “first growth” as automatically “the best today”. The ranks are historically formed, and actual quality and prices change with vintage, management and market.

A more reliable reading is to see the list as a commercial archive of 19th-century Bordeaux trade. It shows how a market built order through prices, reputation and brokers' networks before modern rating agencies existed; it also warns that once a hierarchy is fixed in text, it begins to drift away from a constantly changing reality.
