The Natural Wine Movement: From Beaujolais Experiments to a 2020 Label

From the 1970s, a group of winemakers in French Beaujolais, influenced by the chemist Jules Chauvet, experimented with fermentation without added sulphur and with indigenous yeasts; the "Gang of Four", formed around 1980, brought the approach to market. An industry association was founded in 2019, its charter was recognised by the French authorities in 2020, and Vin Méthode Nature became the first state-recognised natural wine label.

Natural wine usually means wine made with as little intervention as possible: grapes from organic or ecological farming, indigenous yeasts, no winemaking additives, and no filtration, centrifugation or heat treatment; sulphur is kept very low or omitted altogether. In the second half of the 20th century a small group of winemakers in French Beaujolais tested this approach repeatedly, and it gradually grew from a local practice into an international trend. In 2020 it received, for the first time, a label name recognised by the French state: Vin Méthode Nature.

Image: Map of the Beaujolais vineyard, showing appellations and villages. Credit: Cyril5555, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons (source page). The map provides regional context for the natural wine movement; it is not an on-site photograph of the event.

Date and place

The movement began in Beaujolais, in eastern-central France, particularly around Morgon. This is Gamay country and the home of Beaujolais Nouveau, and it is where the chemist and wine merchant Jules Chauvet worked. In the 1970s and 1980s his younger followers tried sulphur-free winemaking in Morgon, Fleurie and neighbouring villages; the practice later spread to the Loire, the Jura and the Rhône. The label recognised in 2020 is national, and applies to any qualifying French wine, not only to Beaujolais.

Background: the industrialisation of post-war wine

Post-war French wine moved towards standardisation: high-yielding varieties, chemical herbicides and pesticides, selected commercial yeasts, filtration and centrifugation, and sulphur dioxide to control oxidation and microbes. This made wine more stable and more consistent, and it also flattened differences between vintages and sites. Beaujolais Nouveau became a commercial success in the second half of the 20th century, but its high yields and uniform style also became a counter-example.

Chauvet was both a merchant and a researcher. He studied malolactic fermentation, carbonic maceration and aromatic compounds, and argued that with healthy grapes and rigorous hygiene, wine could be stable with little or no added sulphur. His conclusion was conditional rather than dogmatic: the fruit had to be ripe and clean, and the containers and operations had to be strict. That technical position later became the methodological basis of the natural wine movement.

Course: four growers and a nickname

Marcel Lapierre took over his family domaine in Morgon in 1973. From the late 1970s into the 1980s he gradually adopted Chauvet's methods: late harvest, strict sorting, whole clusters or carbonic maceration, indigenous yeasts, cool fermentation and no sulphur. Around 1980 he formed a small circle with Jean Foillard, Guy Breton and Jean-Paul Thévenet, later called the "Gang of Four" by the American importer Kermit Lynch. It was not a formal organisation but a group of neighbours who shared methods and tasted together.

Their wines reached markets in the United States, Japan and northern Europe through importers, and found followers among restaurants and merchants focused on flavour. Natural wine later acquired its own fairs, bars and broker networks; by the 2010s, Paris, London, Tokyo and Copenhagen all had venues identified with natural wine, and disagreements over price and style grew alongside.

Impact and institutions

Natural wine had no legal definition for a long time. French authorities even prohibited the commercial use of vin nature or vin naturel on labels, because such words suggested a quality grade that had not been regulated. The trade therefore needed a verifiable name.

In 2019 a group of winemakers organised and founded the Syndicat de défense des vins naturels, drafting the Vin Méthode Nature charter; in 2020 the charter was recognised by the French competition, consumer and fraud authority (DGCCRF). The core rules include: 100% of grapes from certified organic or Nature & Progrès farming; entirely manual harvest; indigenous yeasts only; no winemaking additives; no chaptalisation, concentration, thermovinification or other operations that alter the constitution of the grapes; a ban on reverse osmosis, filtration, centrifugation and flash pasteurisation; and no sulphur before or during fermentation, with total sulphur in the finished wine not exceeding 30 mg/L.

The label uses two logos: a version with no added sulphur, which allows up to 20 mg/L of naturally occurring sulphur in the finished wine, and a version with a small sulphur addition, capped at 30 mg/L. Each cuvée must be declared before the end of the year, and at bottling the producer submits six COFRAC-accredited analyses for internal review by the association; about 1% of cuvées are checked externally each year, and cuvées above 100 hectolitres are checked mandatorily.

Sources and disputes

First, definitions are not uniform. The label covers only producers who sign the charter; unaffiliated growers may still call what they do natural wine, but the word cannot be used as a formal commercial label in France. What a consumer sees as "natural" may mean no additives at all, or "as few as possible".

Second, the history is told with a legendary tone. Chauvet's contribution is supported by technical writing, but "he invented natural wine alone" is an oversimplification; sulphur-free winemaking had local traditions in the south of France, the Jura and Italy. The Gang of Four was a nickname given by an importer, and the members themselves may not have used it.

Third, dates vary. Lapierre took over the domaine in 1973, and the sulphur-free experiments are often dated to around 1980; the month of the association's founding and of the charter's recognition differ between industry articles, so this essay gives only the years.

Fourth, state recognition is not the same as a compulsory appellation-style standard. It is a regulated labelling term, compliance depends on voluntary membership and association review, and external sampling is limited; it is not an independent guarantee for every bottle.

Fifth, health and quality claims lack consistent evidence. Lower sulphur may reduce the risk of reactions in some people, but it can also bring oxidation, volatile acidity and microbial problems; "natural" does not mean safer, better tasting or suitable for everyone.

Timeline

  • 1907: Jules Chauvet is born.
  • From the 1950s: Chauvet studies fermentation, indigenous yeasts and the role of sulphur in Beaujolais.
  • 1973: Marcel Lapierre takes over the family domaine in Morgon.
  • Around 1980: the Gang of Four forms and sulphur-free wine reaches the market.
  • 1989: Chauvet dies.
  • 2019: the natural wine association is founded and the charter takes shape.
  • 2020: the charter is recognised by the DGCCRF and the Vin Méthode Nature label is launched.
