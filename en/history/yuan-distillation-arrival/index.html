The Yuan Arrival of Distilled Liquor: Araq and Shaojiu

In the Yuan period (1271–1368) distilled liquor arrived from the Western Regions under the name araq, also written halaqi and several other ways, generally taken as a transcription of Arabic 'araq. It was made by heating fermented mash and collecting the distillate, giving far higher strength than fermented wine. It entered the court and wealthy households first, then spread more widely. Li Shizhen's judgement that shaojiu was 'not an ancient method, first created in the Yuan' became the main line of later accounts, though the date of earlier artefacts is still disputed.

The Yuan period (1271–1368) marks a key technical turn in Chinese wine history. Before it, fermented wine dominated: once the alcohol in the mash reached about 20%, the yeast stopped working, and the finished wine was usually only a dozen or so percent alcohol. Distilled liquor, which arrived from the Western Regions in the Yuan, heated the mash in a 'water-fire cauldron' or a steamer and collected the condensed distillate; the strength could reach 60% or more. Contemporaries called it araq, also written halaqi, zhalai ji, aliqi and alangqi; researchers generally take the name as a transcription of Arabic 'araq.

Date and place

The records of the arrival cluster in the Yuan, especially in the 14th century. Hu Sihui's Yinshan Zhengyao, completed in 1330, says 'steam good wine and collect the dew to make araq'. Xu Youren's Zhizheng Ji says: 'In the world people use a water-fire cauldron to refine wine and collect the dew; the vapour is fierce and clear, and the autumn sky's evening mist is no better. Its method comes from the Western Regions; from the imperial household it reached noble families, and now it has spread across the world.' Ye Ziqi, writing at the turn of the Yuan and Ming, recalled: 'For method-wine, one uses a vessel to take the essence of the wine by burning, and it is called halaqi. The wine is extremely strong, clear as water, and is indeed wine dew... these are all method-wines of the Yuan dynasty, which did not exist in ancient times.'

On place, 'its method comes from the Western Regions' is common to several records: it entered the court and wealthy households first and then spread to the wider population. 'Western Regions' here is a Yuan term for a broad area of Central and West Asia and should not be equated directly with any single modern country; the Chinese materials do not allow the specific route and intermediate steps to be reconstructed stop by stop.

On archaeology, the Yuan shaojiu workshop site at Lidu town, Jinxian county, Nanchang, Jiangxi, was named one of the top ten Chinese archaeological discoveries of 2002 and became a sixth-batch national key cultural relic protection unit in 2006. The site contains Yuan wine cellars and Ming workshop remains, giving material evidence that shaojiu was produced in the Yuan; but it proves local production, not by itself the route by which the technique arrived.

Background: the strength ceiling of fermented wine

Before distillation, the main body of Chinese wine was fermented wine of the huangjiu type. Grain was saccharified by qu and fermented by yeast, but rising alcohol concentration inhibited the yeast, so the mash had a natural ceiling. Sun Ji notes that once the alcohol in the mash reached 20% the yeast stopped fermenting, that fermented wine generally contained around 18%, and that distillation could raise the alcohol content to 60% or more. This chemical constraint explains why distillation was not a choice for 'better taste' but a technique for raising strength, extending keeping quality and using up sour or spoiled mash.

The Yuan arrival of distilled liquor is therefore not an isolated curiosity but the next step after a very long fermented-wine tradition: qu, mash, vessels and drinking rituals were already mature; what was missing was the step that separated the alcohol from the mash.

Course: the Yuan records of araq

The Yuan records are remarkably consistent: 'good wine' or 'strong wine with lees' was put into a steamer, heated so that vapour rose, and the dripping distillate was collected. It was 'fierce and clear' in vapour and 'clear as water', a sharp contrast to the cloudy body of huangjiu. Xu Youren says it went 'from the imperial household to noble families, and now has spread across the world', which suggests that by the later Yuan it had moved from a court curiosity to a fairly common commodity.

Early drinkers did not respond only with praise. The Yinshan Zhengyao calls it 'very heating and highly toxic'; the Jinshi Zhi says 'halaqi poisons people especially'; the Caomuzi even says 'drinking it makes one pass out and die'. These statements reflect the unfamiliarity with, and fear of, high-strength liquor, and show that it was first understood as a medicine, stimulant or luxury rather than an everyday table wine.

Name and origin

'Araq' is the core of a family of transcriptions: halaqi, zhalai ji, aliqi and alangqi are the same foreign word recorded differently in different texts. Sun Ji takes it as a transcription of Arabic 'araq. Two things need separating: the foreignness of the name shows that the word came from the Western Regions, but it cannot by itself prove that the technique too could only have come from there; the spread of techniques and the spread of words are not always in step.

Another common misunderstanding equates araq directly with today's baijiu. In the Yuan and Ming, shaojiu, huijiu and araq jiu were general terms for distilled liquor, and the raw material could be glutinous rice, japonica rice, millet, sorghum or barley; modern baijiu is classified by aroma type, a concept formed later. This essay therefore treats shaojiu as a technical category, not as the origin story of one aroma type.

Technique: from mash to wine dew

The Yuan records give the skeleton of the process: 'steam and boil to collect the dew', heating an alcoholic mash so that alcohol and water vaporise, then condensing and collecting them. The vessels mentioned, the 'water-fire cauldron' and the 'steamer', show that distilling equipment borrowed from and adapted existing cooking and brewing vessels.

Li Shizhen in the Ming described it in more detail: 'Put strong wine and lees into a steamer, heat so that the vapour rises, and use a vessel to catch the dripping dew. Any sour or spoiled wine can be steamed and burned.' This sentence carries two important points: distillation could rescue sour mash, and the object distilled was 'strong wine and lees', that is, fermented mash with or without solids. It has a lineage connection with the later 'solid-state distillation' and steamer-basket distillation of baijiu, but the Yuan materials are not detailed enough to reconstruct the process at the level of individual steps.

Influence

First, it changed the ceiling of alcoholic strength. Fermented wine was a dozen or so percent alcohol, distilled liquor could exceed 60%, and the measurement, vessels and etiquette of drinking changed with it; small cups, slow drinking and accounting by the liang are connected with the spread of strong liquor.

Second, it changed the uses of wine. Early records treated araq as a medicine and stimulant, and only later did it enter the banquet. Medicinal wine, infused wine and shaojiu became increasingly connected in the literature after the Yuan.

Third, it prepared the ground for the localisation of shaojiu in the Ming and Qing. The Yuan was more about arrival and naming; the Ming was about diffusion and differentiation: in the north it was drunk in all seasons, in the south mostly in summer, and huangjiu remained the star of the banquet. That turn is taken up on this site's Ming shaojiu page.

Fourth, it left a set of cross-language technical terms. Araq, halaqi, shaojiu and huijiu are at once a history of names and evidence of technical exchange.

Sources and disputes

First, the documentary chain for the Yuan view is strong, but it is not an archaeological verdict. Sun Ji lists five sources, the Yinshan Zhengyao, Zhizheng Ji, Caomuzi, Bencao Gangmu and Wuli Xiaoshi, and notes that their authors were writing about their own or recent times, with one voice, so their authority cannot be ignored. But documentary agreement is not the same as proof that no still existed before.

Second, the date of earlier objects is disputed. A set of bronze distillation vessels was unearthed at Xishanzui in Qinglong county, Chengde, Hebei. Some scholars dated it to the Jin and argued that distilled liquor already existed in the Song and Jin; Sun Ji notes that an accompanying drip tile with floral scrollwork resembles tiles from the Yuan residential site at Houyingfang alley inside Xizhimen, Beijing, and therefore dates the still to the Yuan as well. This essay follows Sun Ji's dating but does not present the dispute as closed.

Third, modern brand narratives often trace their origins back to the Han and Tang. Sun Ji says bluntly that 'distilled liquor did not yet exist then, so there is nowhere to claim kinship'. Such claims are commercial storytelling, not historical sources, and are not used here.

Fourth, the Yuan records come mostly from elite texts, and the extent of popular use cannot be quantified. 'Spread across the world' is rhetoric, not evidence of market coverage; judging actual Yuan output and consumption needs more local archives and archaeological material.

Timeline

  • 1271: the Yuan dynasty is founded, forming the social setting for the distilled-liquor records.
  • 1330: the Yinshan Zhengyao is completed, recording 'steam good wine and collect the dew to make araq'.
  • 14th century: the Zhizheng Ji records that 'its method comes from the Western Regions; from the imperial household it reached noble families, and now it has spread across the world'.
  • Late Yuan and early Ming: the Caomuzi recalls that 'halaqi... these are all method-wines of the Yuan dynasty, which did not exist in ancient times'.
  • 1368: the Yuan falls and the Ming rises; shaojiu enters the Ming wine system.
  • 1593: the Bencao Gangmu is printed, and its shaojiu entry concludes that 'shaojiu is not an ancient method; its method was first created in the Yuan'.
