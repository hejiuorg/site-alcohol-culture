Song Wine Tax: State Offices, Private Quotas and Fiscal Dependence

The Song called its revenue from the wine monopoly and wine taxes 'wine tax' (jiuke). Prefectural cities set up state offices to brew, counties, towns and villages were allowed to brew against a fixed annual quota, and the three capitals had state-made qu. Wine revenue rose from 4.28 million strings in 1006 to 17.1 million strings in 1045, becoming a major part of commercial taxation.

The Song called its revenue from the wine monopoly and wine taxes 'wine tax' (jiuke). A single sentence in the Song History sets out the frame: 'In prefectural cities offices are everywhere set up to brew wine; in counties, towns, wards and villages the people may be allowed to brew, with their annual quota fixed; where extra profit remains, local authorities often ask for state selling. In the three capitals the state makes qu, and the people may obtain it by paying the price.' State offices, private quotas and state-made qu together formed a network covering city and countryside, and wine revenue grew from a local miscellaneous levy into a major item of state finance.

Date and place

The system covered the whole Song period, but its intensity varied by region. Prefectural cities had 'offices' where the state brewed and sold directly; counties, towns and villages allowed private brewing against an annual quota fixed by the state; the three capitals had state-made qu bought by the people. Chen, Hua, Cai, Ying, Sui, Ying, Deng, Jin and Fang prefectures and the Xinyang army had traditionally not monopolised wine; at the start of the Taiping Xingguo era the transport commissioner of Jingxi, Cheng Neng, asked to introduce the monopoly, setting up officials and offices and using state money to buy fuel and pay wages. The annual return was tiny, the state wine was poor, and households were even assessed for wedding and funeral wine according to size; when Emperor Taizong learned of the abuses, in 994 he ordered recruitment of private brewers. This episode shows that the state monopoly was not a single nationwide command but a set of measures repeatedly tested and adjusted locally.

Background: monopoly and commercial taxation since the mid-Tang

After the mid-Tang, the equal-field system and the militia system collapsed, the shift to paid soldiers brought sustained military costs, and monopoly and commercial taxation rose in fiscal importance. The Song 'largely inherited the Tang system of fiscal goods', taking over the monopoly of salt, tea and wine along with commercial taxes and institutionalising them. In the first year of Xianping, Emperor Zhenzong ordered the Three Finance Commissions to 'plan tea, salt, wine and tax revenue to meet annual expenditure', turning monopoly and commercial taxation from a supplement to the two land taxes into a principal source. Wine revenue was counted, compared and assessed within this framework.

System: offices, annual quotas and qu money

The state 'office' was the brewing and selling institution of a prefectural city. It had both workshops and shops, and sold directly to urban consumers; revenue was paid against a quota, and local officials' performance was compared with the 'ancestral quota' and the 'previous term', that is, with the actual income of the preceding period.

Private brewing worked through 'fixing the annual quota': brewing was permitted, but a yearly payment was first fixed. In 994 the court 'ordered the recruitment of private brewers, who would pay the state two-thirds less than the normal quota', required applicants' property to be inspected, and made the senior officials and leading local families joint guarantors, so that a shortfall in the quota was shared and repaid. This arrangement shifted part of the operating risk to the contractor and guarantors while locking in revenue for the state.

Qu was another revenue line. In the three capitals the state made qu, and the people 'paid the price to obtain it', meaning they had to buy qu before brewing; in the Sichuan and Shaanxi regions the price of qu was once high and was reduced, and later monopoly was revived, with policy swinging back and forth. Qu money and wine revenue were accounted separately, forming two charging methods within one system.

Influence: why wine revenue mattered

First, it changed the fiscal structure. Li Huarui notes that commercial taxes in the Song greatly exceeded the land tax, and that the monopolies of tea, salt and wine were an important part of this; the article on Chinese Social Sciences Today likewise lists salt, tea and wine monopolies as pillars of Northern Song finance. Wine revenue was not a minor miscellaneous item but a category discussed alongside salt revenue and commercial taxes.

Second, it changed local administration. The prefectural 'office' became a permanent institution, quotas were tied to officials' assessments, and 'comparing revenue with the ancestral quota and the previous term' meant that local officials had to maintain or raise income. An edict of 1007 said that 'from now on no increase of quota may be proposed at court or in the provinces', yet requests for increases kept coming, showing the tension between assessment pressure and the edict.

Third, it changed relations between state and people. Private quotas shifted part of the operating risk to contractors and guarantors; in monopoly areas, assessments fell directly on weddings and funerals. The severity of penalties for private brewing, and the relaxation of collective liability seen in the 846 Tang edict, also existed in the Song, though the material is scattered across penal codes and local records.

Fourth, it supplied institutional vocabulary for later dynasties. 'Office', 'yard', 'quota' and 'annual quota' continued into the Ming and Qing, and discussions of wine tax and monopoly often took the Song as their reference.

Sources and disputes

First, the figures are not on a single basis. Copper cash, iron cash and qu money were counted separately, and conversions between strings and shi or dou varied with region and prices; adding figures from different years and currencies produces a false precision.

Second, Zhang Fangping's '4.28 million to 17.1 million strings' needs to be checked against his original table. Li Huarui's summary establishes the trend but does not set out the circuit-level distribution or the statistical scope; this page treats it only as a trend, not as evidence of per capita consumption.

Third, whether 'allowing the people to brew against a fixed annual quota' equals the later contracting system is still debated. The Song History does not use the term maipu, and although researchers use that concept to describe quota contracting, the material on eligibility, term and profit-and-loss arrangements is incomplete.

Fourth, the coexistence of state offices and private brewing makes 'Song monopoly' hard to define in one sentence. In the same period one prefecture might brew through the state, another through private quotas, another sell qu; policy also swung with famines, military costs and officials' memorials. Treating it as a uniform national monopoly ignores local variation.

Fifth, high wine revenue does not mean high consumption. Prices, population, statistical scope and monopoly profit all affect the figures; the relation between Song wine prices and revenue needs monetary and price research, and revenue alone cannot establish consumption.

Timeline

  • 994: recruitment of private brewers, paying two-thirds below the normal quota, with 472 locations tried first.
  • 996: copper cash, iron cash and capital qu money from the monopoly are recorded separately.
  • 1002: Li Shiheng asks for an increase to support frontier costs, adding 110,000 strings a year.
  • 1006: wine revenue reaches 4.28 million strings, the base for later comparison.
  • 1020: the Liangzhe transport commissioner Fang Zhongxun asks for an increase, adding 98,000 strings a year.
  • 1045: wine revenue rises to 17.1 million strings.
