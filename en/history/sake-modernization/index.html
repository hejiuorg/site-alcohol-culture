The Modernisation of Japanese Sake: Tax, Research Institute and Grades

After the Meiji Restoration Japan brought sake into a unified tax system: the first modern sake tax rules appeared in 1871, the brewing year and licensed volume limits were fixed in 1896, the Brewing Research Institute was founded in 1904, yamahai and sokujo starter methods appeared in 1909 and 1910, and the first national new-sake competition opened in 1911. In the late 20th century the grade system was phased out between 1989 and 1992, and quality labelling shifted to the "special designation" categories.

The modernisation of sake was not a single invention or a single law but a set of parallel changes: the state turned the sake tax into a stable source of revenue and redefined who could brew, how much and on what accounting cycle; research institutions turned fermentation from apprentice knowledge into something that could be analysed and taught; bottling, railways and trademarks brought sake into a national market; and the grade and labelling systems repeatedly rewrote how consumers judged quality. These changes ran from the early Meiji era into the early Heisei era, over more than a century.

Date and place

The institutional changes centred on Tokyo, where the Ministry of Finance, the Brewing Research Institute and later the National Tax Agency made rules and published data. Brewing itself concentrated in two regions: the Nada Five Villages near Kobe, known for Nada sake, and Fushimi in Kyoto, known for a softer style; both sent sake to the Edo and Tokyo markets by water and later by rail. After the Meiji period, breweries in Hiroshima, Akita and Niigata also entered national competition through technique and rice quality. The Brewing Research Institute founded in 1904 concentrated national technical standards and experimental research in a state body.

Background: Edo brewing and Meiji finance

Sake was already a mature industry in the Edo period. Breweries used wooden vats, three-stage preparation and kimoto starters, letting yeast work slowly in winter cold; heating (hiire) and rice polishing made the drink more stable and easier to transport. Nada's kudari-zake reached Edo by water and created brands and price tiers, while guilds and licensed sake shops organised production and sales. Toji were seasonal craftsmen who led brewing in the winter.

After the Meiji Restoration of 1868, the abolition of the domains and the building of a modern state required stable revenue. Sake was widely consumed and easy to tax, so the sake tax became one of the first systematically organised central taxes. The design served two goals at once: raising money and consolidating a large number of small breweries through licences and volume limits.

Course: from licence plates to the Sake Tax Act

In 1871, in the fourth year of Meiji, the government issued the Rules for Licensing and Taxing the Brewing of Sake, Unrefined Sake and Soy Sauce. This was the first nationwide modern sake tax code, but National Tax Agency research notes that its content did not differ fundamentally from Edo-period taxation; it mainly brought the old system under the central state. In 1875 the Sake Tax Rules split the tax into a business tax and a brewing tax; a revision in 1878 changed the brewing tax base from sale price to volume brewed, so the object of taxation became output rather than transaction price.

In 1880 the Sake Brewing Tax Rules replaced the Sake Tax Rules, and the business tax was renamed a brewing licence tax. In 1896 the Sake Brewing Tax Act abolished the licence tax, introduced a business tax, and set a licensed volume limit for Japanese sake; the same law defined the brewing year as 1 October to 30 September. In 1899 the Home Brewing Tax Act was repealed and brewery association rules were issued, further narrowing the space for household brewing.

Technical milestones came just as densely. In 1901 Hakutsuru first sold sake in one-sho bottles, making brand and standardised packaging possible. In 1902 the Tanba Toji Association held the country's first brewing-method training course in Sasayama, Hyogo. In 1904 the Ministry of Finance established the Brewing Research Institute, now the National Research Institute of Brewing, bringing fermentation science, yeast and brewing technology into state research. In 1909 Kaga Kinichiro and others developed the yamahai starter; in 1910 Eda Kanjiro proposed the sokujo starter, which greatly shortened starter preparation. In 1911 the institute held the first national new-sake competition, which became a regular channel of technical feedback for breweries.

Impact and institutions

In 1943 grades were introduced by type of alcohol, with sake divided from first to fourth grade; in 1944 the volume-brewed tax was abolished and tax was levied only at shipment. In 1962 alcohol was divided into ten categories and sake grades were simplified to special, first and second. In 1973 preservatives were banned; in 1975 the industry body issued voluntary labelling standards requiring the brewing date, ingredients and method.

In the 1980s ginjo and unpasteurised sake gained popularity, and consumers began to judge quality by process terms such as rice-polishing ratio, junmai and ginjo. In 1989 the tax law abolished grades and the ad valorem tax, with a three-year transition; in 1990 the Standards for the Method and Quality Labelling of Sake were issued, giving the special designation categories a formal definition; in 1992 the grade system was formally retired. Quality communication thus shifted from "grade" to "ingredients and process".

Sources and disputes

First, modernisation is often written as a single line of progress, but it was four interlocking lines: tax, science, packaging and market. Tax law drove consolidation, research institutes drove standardisation, and bottling and railways drove national distribution; they did not always point the same way.

Second, the "modernity" of the 1871 rules is overstated. National Tax Agency research explicitly says they differed little from Edo-period sake tax; the structural changes came in 1875 and 1896.

Third, the year grades ended has two versions. 1989 is when the tax law abolished grades and the ad valorem tax, and 1992 is when the transition ended and grades formally disappeared; giving only one date is misleading.

Fourth, the start of the brewing year later changed. National Tax Agency material shows that from the 1960s the brewing year began on 1 July; yet the industry memory of "1 October as brewing New Year" survives and is used to explain the establishment of Sake Day in 1978.

Fifth, the origin of ginjo is disputed. The technical elements of rice polishing and cool fermentation predate the Meiji era, while ginjo as a product category and market boom became clear only in the late 20th century; naming a single "year ginjo was born" is unsafe.

Timeline

  • 1871: the Rules for Licensing and Taxing Sake, Unrefined Sake and Soy Sauce are issued.
  • 1875: the Sake Tax Rules establish a business tax and a brewing tax.
  • 1880: the Sake Brewing Tax Rules replace the Sake Tax Rules.
  • 1896: the Sake Brewing Tax Act establishes licensed volume limits and the brewing year.
  • 1899: the Home Brewing Tax Act is repealed and brewery association rules are issued.
  • 1901: Hakutsuru sells sake in one-sho bottles.
  • 1902: the Tanba Toji Association holds the first national brewing-method course.
  • 1904: the Ministry of Finance establishes the Brewing Research Institute.
  • 1909: the yamahai starter is developed; 1910: the sokujo starter is proposed.
  • 1911: the first national new-sake competition is held.
  • 1943: sake is graded from first to fourth grade.
  • 1962: grades are simplified to special, first and second.
  • 1973: preservatives are banned.
  • 1978: the Japan Sake and Shochu Makers Association establishes Sake Day.
  • 1982: ginjo and unpasteurised sake gain popularity.
  • 1989: the tax law abolishes grades and the ad valorem tax with a three-year transition.
  • 1990: the Standards for the Method and Quality Labelling of Sake are issued.
  • 1992: the grade system is formally abolished.
