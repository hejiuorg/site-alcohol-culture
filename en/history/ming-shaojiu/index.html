Ming Shaojiu: From Medicine to Northern Everyday Drink

Ming shaojiu (1368–1644) was no longer the foreign strong liquor that had just arrived in the Yuan, but it had not yet replaced huangjiu. Banquets still favoured huangjiu, and shaojiu appeared more often in northern everyday life, in medical formulas, in travel and in summer drinking. Li Shizhen gave shaojiu a separate entry in the Bencao Gangmu, recording both the 'steam and collect the dew' process and the warning of 'great toxicity'; that text later became the core basis for accounts of Ming shaojiu.

Date and place

Ming records of shaojiu cluster in the 16th century, that is, the Jiajing and Wanli reigns. Li Shizhen (1518–1593) completed the Bencao Gangmu in 1578 and it was printed in 1593; its shaojiu entry gathered the previously scattered records into one piece.

Regional difference is the key to understanding Ming shaojiu. The Bencao Gangmu says 'northerners drink it in all seasons, southerners only in summer': in the north it was a year-round drink, in the south mostly a summer one. Yang Zhishui notes that distilled liquor came from the Western Regions in the Yuan under the name halaqi, but that by the Ming, huangjiu still dominated banquets north and south. Gu Qiyuan's Kezuo Zhuilv, chapter 9, says 'officials and scholars used only Jinhua wine', reflecting the choice of Jiangnan literati in the mid-to-late Ming and showing that huangjiu's place on formal occasions was not shaken.

On archaeology, the shaojiu workshop site at Lidu town, Jinxian county, Nanchang, Jiangxi, contains both Yuan cellars and Ming workshop remains; it was named one of the top ten Chinese archaeological discoveries of 2002 and became a sixth-batch national key cultural relic protection unit in 2006. It shows that shaojiu production continued through the Ming, but a single site cannot represent national output.

Background: huangjiu first, shaojiu second

The basic pattern of Ming wine was huangjiu first and shaojiu second. Huangjiu used glutinous and japonica rice, was saccharified and fermented with qu, had a lower alcohol content and a mild taste, and suited banquets, gifts and everyday meals; Jinhua wine, Zhejiang wine, Magu wine and southern bean wine are all famous names repeatedly mentioned in the literature.

Shaojiu's position was different. Its strength was high and its bite strong, and it was initially considered 'toxic', so it was more often linked with medicine, driving out cold, drying damp and travel. Ming medical books used shaojiu as a medicine rather than a simple drink; this also explains why the earliest shaojiu records are concentrated in medical works and materia medica rather than banquet accounts.

Course: the Bencao Gangmu entry

The structure of the shaojiu entry in chapter 25 of the Bencao Gangmu is clear. The naming section begins 'fire wine, araq wine'; the nature section says 'pungent, sweet, very heating, highly toxic'; the collected explanations say 'shaojiu is not an ancient method; its method was first created in the Yuan', 'clear as water, extremely strong in flavour, it is wine dew', and 'northerners drink it in all seasons, southerners only in summer'; finally it warns that 'drinking without restraint kills within moments'.

The value of this text is that it records names, process, uses and risks at once. It rewrites shaojiu from a 'foreign curiosity' into 'a wine with a fixed method', and turns the scattered reports since the Yuan into knowledge that could be repeated. Almost every later discussion of the origin of Chinese distilled liquor begins with this sentence.

Technique: steam and collect the dew

The collected explanations in the Bencao Gangmu give the process as 'put strong wine and lees into a steamer, heat so that the vapour rises, and use a vessel to catch the dripping dew. Any sour or spoiled wine can be steamed and burned'. It then adds that 'in recent times only glutinous rice, or japonica rice, or millet, or sorghum, or barley is steamed, mixed with qu and fermented in a jar... and distilled in a steamer'.

This description carries three points: the raw material was not limited to one grain; the fermentation stage was shared with huangjiu, all of it 'steam—mix with qu—put in a jar'; distillation was an extra step after fermentation. Compared with the Yuan record of refining wine in a 'water-fire cauldron', it is closer to the later 'steamer-basket distillation' of baijiu, but the Ming materials are not sufficient to say whether solid-state distillation was already widespread.

Influence: north-south split and the routinisation of strong liquor

First, shaojiu completed the transition 'from medicine to drink' in the Ming. Doctors still treated it as a strong medicine, but it had entered northern everyday drinking; the Bencao Gangmu lists both indications and contraindications, showing that it stood at the boundary between medicine and wine.

Second, north and south drinking habits diverged. 'Northerners drink it in all seasons, southerners only in summer' is not rhetoric but an observation of consumption: the northern climate is cold and strong liquor suited daily use; the south had a deep huangjiu tradition, and shaojiu was more of a seasonal supplement.

Third, huangjiu remained the main drink of banquets and gifts. In Ming novels and notebooks, huangjiu appears far more often than shaojiu on formal occasions; the spread of shaojiu was gradual, not a replacement.

Fourth, it left room for the further localisation of shaojiu in the Qing. Raw materials, qu types and stills diverged by region and eventually formed the aroma-based baijiu system; but that system belongs to the Qing and later, and cannot be projected back as a Ming fact.

Sources and disputes

First, the extent of Ming shaojiu use is easily exaggerated. The surviving materials are mainly medical books, notebooks and novels, without output, tax or consumption statistics; to say that 'Ming shaojiu had already replaced huangjiu' is not supported. This essay positions it only as 'huangjiu first, shaojiu second'.

Second, the regional character of the Jin Ping Mei needs explanation. The story is set in Qinghe, Shandong, under a borrowed name, and its material is mainly northern, yet the book lists Jinhua wine, Zhejiang wine, Magu wine and southern bean wine among many huangjiu; it can reflect the wine structure of a northern market town, but it is not a national sample.

Third, the judgement that 'shaojiu is not an ancient method' is layered. Li Shizhen inherited a Yuan view and did not himself possess earlier still material; the Yuan cellars at Lidu support the existence of Yuan shaojiu production, but also suggest that local production may have had a longer continuous tradition. Documentary judgement and archaeological evidence need to be stated separately.

Fourth, the term 'baijiu' is anachronistic. Ming 'shaojiu', 'fire wine' and 'araq wine' were general terms for distilled liquor with diverse raw materials; modern 'baijiu' is classified by aroma type, a concept formed later. Calling Ming shaojiu 'baijiu' directly would make readers think the aroma system already existed in the Ming.

Timeline

  • 1368: the Ming is founded, and shaojiu production continues on the Yuan foundation.
  • 1578: the Bencao Gangmu is completed, including the shaojiu entry.
  • 1593: the Bencao Gangmu is printed, and the shaojiu entry becomes the standard text for later citation.

Begin with the date and setting, move on to the documented consequences, and finish with how later accounts treat it. That order keeps the reading of Ming Shaojiu practical.
