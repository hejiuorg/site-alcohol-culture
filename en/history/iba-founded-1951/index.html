Founding of the International Bartenders Association (1951): A Round Table of Seven Guilds

On 24 February 1951, seven bartenders' associations from Denmark, France, Italy, the Netherlands, Sweden, Switzerland and the United Kingdom decided in the saloon of the Grand Hotel in Torquay, England, to found the International Bartenders Association (IBA), with Bill Tarling of the UK Bartenders Guild as its first president. The first president was Bill Tarling of the UK Bartenders Guild. The association later connected separate national guilds into a cross-border professional network through the World Cocktail Championship, bartending education and an official cocktail list.

Background

Mid-20th-century bartending organisations were mostly national guilds: the United Kingdom Bartenders Guild in Britain, the Association des Barmen de France in France and the Swiss Barkeeper Union in Switzerland. Each managed its own members, competitions and apprentices, but stable cross-border channels were lacking. After the war, tourism and the international bar trade recovered, and "how should the same cocktail be made in London, Paris and Copenhagen" became a question that needed a common answer. On 21 February 1951 the Grand Hotel in Torquay hosted a European cocktail competition with bartenders from several countries; three days later, on 24 February, guild representatives meeting in the same place decided to create a permanent association. The sequence shows that the IBA grew out of a competition and an industry gathering rather than a declaration.

Course and timeline

  • 21 February 1951: the Grand Hotel in Torquay hosts a European cocktail competition, providing people and topics for the founding meeting.
  • 24 February 1951: representatives of seven guilds from Denmark, France, Italy, the Netherlands, Sweden, Switzerland and the United Kingdom decide in the Grand Hotel saloon to found the IBA; Bill Tarling becomes first president.
  • 1952: George Baker succeeds as president; George Sieve of Switzerland, Pietro Grandi of Italy and Kurt Sorensen of Denmark follow.
  • October 1955: the first International Cocktail Competition is held in Amsterdam, the forerunner of the World Cocktail Championship.
  • 1963–1976: Angelo Zola of Italy serves as president, having been one of the founding round-table participants, and stabilises the competition and education system.
  • 1976: membership reaches 30 countries.
  • 1999: the event is renamed the World Cocktail Championship; in 2011 it becomes the World Cocktail Championships.
  • Present: the association website lists 64 member countries and 3 observers.

Key people and institutions

Bill Tarling represented the UK Bartenders Guild and was the first president; Angelo Zola led the association from 1963 to 1976 and helped stabilise competitions and education. The French bartenders' association, the Swiss Barkeeper Union and the Italian, Danish, Dutch and Swedish guilds formed the founding network. The Grand Hotel in Torquay was the founding venue, but it was a seaside resort hotel rather than a famous cocktail bar, which suggests the meeting was closer to an industry association annual general meeting than a star bartender's personal project. The IBA's organisational form is a federation of national guilds; its daily work is to connect members, run competitions, provide education and issue industry positions.

Concrete changes for the industry

First, it created a cross-border competition system. The World Cocktail Championship brings national champions to one venue and judges taste, technique and presentation, making it a career milestone for many bartenders. Second, it promoted standardised education. Association courses standardised recipes, hygiene and service flow, turning bartending from a bar job into a professional identity that can be described and assessed. Third, it published an official cocktail list. The list groups recipes into "Unforgettables", "Contemporary Classics" and "New Era Drinks", giving ingredients, proportions and methods for national guilds and competitions to use. Fourth, it built an industry network. Membership reached 30 countries in 1976, and the current website lists 64 member countries, close to a trade federation in scale. Fifth, it influenced bar operations. The official list and competition style became references for menu design, training and purchasing, and multinational spirits brands used the events to enter national markets.

Disputes and later assessments

First, the founding details rely on the association's own account. The 1951 meeting records are not widely public, and the list of founding countries, the number of attendees and the first president are confirmed mainly by the association website and commemorative texts from member guilds. Second, the authority of the official list is limited. It is an industry reference, not a mandatory standard; proportions and garnishes may change between versions, and competitions do not follow it exactly. Third, names and translations are inconsistent. Chinese-language sources use several renderings of the association's name, and the abbreviation IBA can be confused with other bodies, so the full name should be given. Fourth, claims of uniqueness are self-description. The IBA is not the only non-profit international bartending organisation, and promotional language should not be treated as independent fact. Fifth, membership figures differ: some sources say 62 countries, others 64, possibly reflecting different membership categories or statistical dates.

Common misreadings

First, the IBA is not a regulator. It does not issue legally binding bartending licences or force bars in any country to follow its recipes. Second, the official cocktail list is not a legal standard but an industry reference; versions and competitions differ. Third, the IBA was not founded in London, Paris or New York, but in the English seaside town of Torquay. Fourth, the first worldwide competition was not in 1951 but in Amsterdam in 1955; 1951 was the year the association was founded. Fifth, the IBA is not the only international bartending body; calling it "the only" one is self-description and should not be treated as independent fact.
