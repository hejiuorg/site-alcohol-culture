US Prohibition: The Experiment and Repeal of 1920 to 1933

The Eighteenth Amendment and the Volstead Act, in force from 1920, banned the manufacture, sale and transportation of alcoholic beverages; weak enforcement brought bootlegging, speakeasies and organized crime. The Twenty-first Amendment repealed Prohibition in 1933 and returned more alcohol regulation to the states.

1976 Judgment of Paris: California wine beats French estates

On 24 May 1976 the British wine merchant Steven Spurrier organised a blind tasting in Paris; 20 wines were poured, 10 from California and 10 from France, and California wines took the top score in both the red and white groups. The event later called the Judgment of Paris changed the international standing of California wine and became a classic case of cross-region comparison.

The 2012 baijiu plasticiser incident

In November 2012 a sample of Jiugui liquor was found to contain 1.08 mg/kg of the plasticiser dibutyl phthalate (DBP), above the 0.3 mg/kg reference limit then applied, triggering a crisis of trust and nationwide checks across the baijiu industry. This essay explains the course, the standards gap, the remediation and the longer effects.

1952 National Wine Evaluation: the eight famous wines and the start of a system

The first national wine evaluation, held in Beijing in September 1952, selected eight Chinese famous wines from 103 samples; the list included four baijiu, Shaoxing jiafan wine and three Zhangyu products, setting the framework for later national evaluations and famous-wine branding.

A History of Korean Soju: From Distilled Spirit to the Green Bottle

Korean soju has two lineages: a grain-distilled spirit shaped by exchanges between Goryeo and the Mongol Yuan from the 13th century, still represented by Andong soju, and a diluted soju created after the 1965 Grain Management Act restricted rice in brewing, made by diluting alcohol distilled from imported starch. The latter took the mass market in green bottles at low prices and low strength, and became a nationwide industrial category after production restrictions ended in 1996.

A Tax History of Scotch Whisky: From the 1644 Excise to the 1823 Excise Act

In 1644 the Scottish Parliament first taxed whisky, opening a period of more than 150 years of illicit distilling and smuggling. By the 1820s as many as 14,000 illicit stills were seized each year and more than half the whisky drunk was untaxed. On 18 July 1823 the Excise Act replaced prohibition with a £10 licence and duty per gallon, making legal distilling profitable, and is widely seen as the start of the modern Scotch whisky industry.

Craft Beer Revolution (1978): US Homebrewing Legalization and the Microbrewery

On 14 October 1978 President Jimmy Carter signed the tax bill H.R. 1337, whose Cranston-Steiger amendment removed the federal excise tax on beer brewed at home; the provision took effect on 1 February 1979. Homebrewing became lawful again, and homebrewers, supply shops and small breweries fed one another in what is often called the start of the American craft beer revolution.

Founding of the International Bartenders Association (1951): A Round Table of Seven Guilds

On 24 February 1951, seven bartenders' associations from Denmark, France, Italy, the Netherlands, Sweden, Switzerland and the United Kingdom decided in the saloon of the Grand Hotel in Torquay, England, to found the International Bartenders Association (IBA), with Bill Tarling of the UK Bartenders Guild as its first president. The association later connected separate national guilds into a cross-border professional network through the World Cocktail Championship, bartending education and an official cocktail list.

Ming Shaojiu: From Medicine to Northern Everyday Drink

In the Ming, shaojiu was no longer the foreign strong liquor of its first Yuan appearance, but it had not yet replaced huangjiu. Banquets still favoured huangjiu, while shaojiu appeared more often in northern everyday drinking, in medical formulas and in summer use. Li Shizhen's Bencao Gangmu gave shaojiu its own entry, recording the 'steam and collect the dew' process and warning of its 'great toxicity', and that entry became the core text for later accounts of Ming shaojiu.

Qing Huangjiu: Shaoxing Wine Reaches the Whole Country

The Qing period (1644–1911) was the high point of Shaoxing huangjiu. The Kuaiji county gazetteer of the Kangxi reign already spoke of 'Yue wine travelling the world', and in the Daoguang reign Liang Zhangju called Shaoxing wine 'current throughout the seas, the true orthodox wine', shipped as far as Xinjiang. Breweries, wine houses and the 'Beijing parcel' and 'Guangdong parcel' distribution network turned huangjiu from a local product into a national commodity.

Shang and Zhou Wine Ritual: Bronze Vessels, the Wine Officer and the Jiugao

Drinking in the Shang and Zhou periods was embedded in sacrifice, banqueting and hierarchy. Bronze wine-vessel sets, the duties of Zhou officials such as the wine officer, and the limits on drinking in the Book of Documents' Jiugao together make up what 'wine ritual' meant. This essay sets out what each line of evidence can prove, and why the Rites of Zhou and the Jiugao need to be read with caution.

Song Wine Tax: State Offices, Private Quotas and Fiscal Dependence

The Song called its revenue from the wine monopoly and wine taxes 'wine tax' (jiuke). Prefectural cities set up state offices to brew, counties, towns and villages were allowed to brew against a fixed annual quota, and the three capitals had state-made qu. Wine revenue rose from 4.28 million strings in 1006 to 17.1 million strings in 1045, becoming a major part of commercial taxation.

State-owned distilleries and the alcohol monopoly after 1949

After 1949 private distilleries were taken over, merged and gradually converted into state-owned or joint state-private plants, while alcohol was brought under a monopoly system. This essay explains the timing, the institutional arrangements and the effect on the structure of the industry.

Tang Wine Workshops: Licensed Houses, State Workshops and State Shops

In the early Tang, brewing was mainly private and prohibition came in famine years; after the An Lushan rebellion fiscal pressure rose, licensed wine households were fixed in 764, state brewing was ordered in 782, excise was imposed in the capital region in 786, and in the Yuanhe period the wine levy was folded into the two taxes. Wine-making thus split into taxed households, state workshops and state shops.

The 1516 Bavarian Reinheitsgebot: How a Local Ordinance Became a National Symbol

On 23 April 1516 Duke Wilhelm IV and Duke Ludwig X of Bavaria issued a Landesordnung at Ingolstadt requiring beer to be made from barley, hops and water. It was a Bavarian local ordinance, not a German national law, and yeast was not mentioned. Only in 1906 did its scope extend across Germany; it later became a powerful symbol of German brewing.

The 1855 Bordeaux Classification: A List Produced for a World's Fair

On 18 April 1855, Bordeaux brokers delivered a classification requested by the Chamber of Commerce, ranking Médoc and Graves red wines and Sauternes and Barsac sweet wines into growths. It rested on long-term market prices and reputation, not a blind tasting, and has seen only two official changes since.

The 1927 Champagne Delimitation: Names, Parcels and a Boundary War

On 22 July 1927 France passed a law formally delimiting the Champagne vineyard: 407 communes, based on the Marne and Aisne communes fixed by the 1908 decree, plus three communes in the Aube and those that had claimed the Champagne name in harvest declarations from 1919 to 1924. The law recognised only land already planted, or planted before the phylloxera crisis, turning a departmental dispute into a parcel dispute. Over-registration in 1928 and a review from 1951 eventually cut the area to about 34,000 hectares and 302 communes.

The 1982 Bordeaux Vintage: How a Year Became a Turning Point in the Modern Wine Market

Bordeaux in 1982 had a mild winter, early budbreak, a hot July and a dry September, producing ripe, rich wines. Some American critics initially thought the wines were too ripe and low in acidity; Robert Parker championed the vintage in The Wine Advocate, and over time 1982 became a turning point for his reputation and for the Bordeaux en primeur market. It was not a uniformly perfect vintage, and differences among estates and appellations were large.

The Boston Molasses Flood of 1919: An Industrial-Alcohol Disaster

On 15 January 1919 a storage tank holding about 2.3 million gallons of molasses burst in Boston's North End. The molasses moved into the streets at an estimated 35 mph, killing 21 people and injuring 150. The tank belonged to Purity Distilling, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, and supplied material for wartime industrial alcohol. The disaster prompted reflection on tank design, construction and urban safety.

The Champagne Riots of 1911: Appellation Boundaries, Grape Prices and the Identity of Champagne

In 1911 Champagne was shaken by large-scale protest over appellation boundaries and grape prices. The 1908 administrative delimitation excluded most of the Aube from Champagne, angering local growers; in April 1911 growers in the Marne attacked merchants suspected of buying Aube grapes. The riots produced a temporary "second Champagne zone" and helped shape the 1927 delimitation.

The Cocktail Revival (1980s): From the Rainbow Room to Craft Bartending

In the 1970s sweet pre-mixes and disco culture pushed classic recipes into the background. In 1987, when the Rainbow Room reopened in New York, Dale DeGroff returned to Jerry Thomas's 1862 approach: fresh juice, house-made syrup, large ice and a restrained menu. That revival, framed as getting the classics right, grew into the craft cocktail movement of the 1990s.

The Dukang Wine Legend: How a Person Became a Synonym for Wine

The Eastern Han Shuowen Jiezi records that 'Dukang made shu wine' and adds that 'Shaokang is Dukang'; Cao Cao's line 'what relieves sorrow? Only Dukang' turned the name into a synonym for wine. At least four accounts of Dukang's era, identity and brewing place circulate in texts and local traditions; this essay separates sources, scholarly views and legend.

The Founding of Changyu (1892): The Start of Modern Chinese Winemaking

In 1892 the overseas Chinese businessman Zhang Bishi invested 3 million taels of silver to found the Changyu Winery at Zhifu Bay in Yantai, Shandong, widely regarded as the start of industrial winemaking in China. After three rounds of vine introduction and three attempts to hire a winemaker, the company built an underground cellar between 1894 and 1905, produced wine in 1899 and brandy in 1914, and won a grand prize at the 1915 Panama-Pacific exposition.

The Founding of the Qingdao Brewery (1903): German and British Merchants and China's First Continuously Operating Brewery

On 15 August 1903 German and British merchants founded the Germania Brewery Company, Qingdao, at what is now 56 Dengzhou Road, with initial annual output of about 2000 tonnes of pale and dark beer. In 1906 its beer won an award at the Munich exhibition; in 1945 the plant was renamed Qingdao Brewery and in 1949 became the state-owned Qingdao Brewery. It is the first Chinese brewery still operating today.

The French Absinthe Ban of 1915: Science, War and the Wine Industry

In the early years of the First World War the Paris police banned the sale of absinthe, and on 16 March 1915 a French law prohibited the production, circulation and sale of absinthe and similar liqueurs. The ban is often blamed on the idea that absinthe caused hallucinations, but modern research shows the fear of thujone was exaggerated; war, nationalism, wine-industry lobbying and the temperance movement were equally important.

The French AOC System (1935): The Birth of Controlled Appellation of Origin

On 30 July 1935 France created the Appellation d'Origine Contrôlée (AOC) by decree and set up a national committee for the appellations of wines and spirits. Half a century of phylloxera, overproduction, adulteration and boundary disputes led to this system, which tied a place name to varieties, yields and methods. It later grew into the INAO system and influenced European origin protection.

The Gin Craze and the Gin Act: Britain's Eighteenth-Century Spirits Crisis

In the first half of the eighteenth century cheap gin spread rapidly through London, creating what contemporaries called the Gin Craze. Licensing, taxation and retail restrictions culminated in the 1751 Gin Act, which, together with Hogarth's Gin Lane, became a turning point in British spirits regulation.

The Han Wine Monopoly: State Trading in 98 BCE and Its Abolition in 81 BCE

In the third year of Tianhan, Emperor Wu 'first established the que wine monopoly', bringing brewing and selling under state control. In the sixth year of Yuanyuan, Emperor Zhao consulted the worthy and learned, and in the seventh month abolished the monopoly officials, replacing direct state trading with licensed private declaration and taxation. Less than twenty years long, it left a model that alternated with monopoly, wine tax and prohibition for centuries.

The Jiahu Alcohol Residues of c. 7000 BCE: The Earliest Chemical Evidence of a Fermented Beverage in China

Chemical analysis of residues in pottery from the Jiahu site in Wuyang, Henan, found features of a mixed fermentation of rice, honey and hawthorn or grape-like fruit, dated as early as about 7000 BCE. The finding pushed direct chemical evidence for Chinese fermented drinks back in time and showed that early brewing was not a single-grain process but a combination of sugar sources.

The London Beer Flood of 1814: Giant Vats, Porter and the St Giles Slum

On 17 October 1814 a giant wooden vat at Meux's Horse Shoe Brewery on Tottenham Court Road burst, sending about 323,000 imperial gallons of porter into the St Giles slum. The wave destroyed houses and a tavern and killed several people. The death toll is usually given as eight; a Historic England archive note counts a ninth person who died the next day of alcohol poisoning.

The Modernisation of Japanese Sake: Tax, Research Institute and Grades

After the Meiji Restoration Japan brought sake into a unified tax system: the first modern sake tax rules appeared in 1871, the brewing year and licensed volume limits were fixed in 1896, the Brewing Research Institute was founded in 1904, yamahai and sokujo starter methods appeared in 1909 and 1910, and the first national new-sake competition opened in 1911. In the late 20th century the grade system was phased out between 1989 and 1992, and quality labelling shifted to the "special designation" categories.

The Nationalisation of Cuban Rum: From the Arechabala Family to Two Havana Clubs

In 1934 the Arechabala family created Havana Club in Cardenas, Cuba. After the 1959 revolution the new government took over the family firm in 1960, and the brand passed to the state agency Cubaexport. In 1993 Cubaexport and Pernod Ricard formed a joint venture that took the Cuban Havana Club global; in 1997 the exiled family sold the US trademark to Bacardi, producing two brands with the same name and litigation that continues.

The Natural Wine Movement: From Beaujolais Experiments to a 2020 Label

From the 1970s, a group of winemakers in French Beaujolais, influenced by the chemist Jules Chauvet, experimented with fermentation without added sulphur and with indigenous yeasts; the "Gang of Four", formed around 1980, brought the approach to market. An industry association was founded in 2019, its charter was recognised by the French authorities in 2020, and Vin Méthode Nature became the first state-recognised natural wine label.

The Phylloxera Epidemic: A Crisis That Reshaped Europe's Vineyards

From the early 1860s, the North American grape phylloxera spread through European vineyards; France confirmed the outbreak around 1863, and the 1870s and 1880s destroyed vast areas. Grafting European vines onto American rootstocks became the main solution, driving replanting, hybrid varieties such as Baco 22A and the reconstruction of wine regions.

The Repeal of Prohibition in 1933: The Twenty-first Amendment and the Rebuilding of American Alcohol Regulation

On 20 February 1933 Congress proposed the Twenty-first Amendment, repealing the Eighteenth. On 5 December 1933 Utah became the thirty-sixth ratifying state, and Acting Secretary of State William Phillips certified the amendment. It is the only U.S. constitutional amendment ratified by state conventions and the only one that fully repeals an earlier amendment. Prohibition ended, but alcohol regulation did not disappear; it shifted to state and local licensing.

The rise of Chinese craft beer around 2015

Around 2015 Chinese craft beer expanded from small bars and breweries in first-tier cities to more cities, with taprooms, beer festivals and domestic brands multiplying. This essay explains the background, the forms, the regulatory conditions and the dispute over scale.

The Whiskey Rebellion of 1791: Federal Taxation and Frontier Politics in Early America

In 1791 the United States imposed an excise tax on distilled spirits. Farmers and distillers in western Pennsylvania resisted with refusals, assemblies and violence. In 1794 Washington mobilised about 13,000 militia and marched west; the rebellion collapsed under military pressure. The Whiskey Rebellion became an early test of federal authority, tax politics and frontier interests.

The Yi Di Wine Legend: 'First to Make Jiu Lao', in Texts of Uncertain Date

Several texts from the Warring States period to the Eastern Han credit Yi Di with 'making wine' or 'making jiu lao', adding the story that Yu drank it, found it sweet, and then distanced himself from Yi Di. These texts were written long after the era the legend describes, and Yi Di's person, identity, and location cannot be verified; this essay treats the story as an ancient explanation of how brewing began.

The Yuan Arrival of Distilled Liquor: Araq and Shaojiu

In the Yuan period (1271–1368) distilled liquor arrived from the Western Regions under the name araq, also written halaqi and several other ways, generally taken as a transcription of Arabic 'araq. It was made by heating fermented mash and collecting the distillate, giving far higher strength than fermented wine. It entered the court and wealthy households first, then spread more widely. Li Shizhen's judgement that shaojiu was 'not an ancient method, first created in the Yuan' became the main line of later accounts, though the date of earlier artefacts is still disputed.
