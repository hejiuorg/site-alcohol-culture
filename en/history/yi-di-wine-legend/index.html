The Yi Di Wine Legend: 'First to Make Jiu Lao', in Texts of Uncertain Date

Several texts from the Warring States period to the Eastern Han credit Yi Di with 'making wine' or 'making jiu lao', adding the story that Yu drank it, found it sweet, and then distanced himself from Yi Di. These texts were written long after the era the legend describes, and Yi Di's person, identity, and location cannot be verified; this essay treats the story as an ancient explanation of how brewing began.

Yi Di is one of the earliest named winemakers in Chinese classical texts. The Shiben, the Lüshi Chunqiu, the Strategies of the Warring States and the Shuowen Jiezi all record that he 'made wine' or 'made jiu lao', and add the story that Yu drank it, found it sweet, and then distanced himself from Yi Di. These texts were written between the Warring States period and the Eastern Han, one or two thousand years after the era the legend points to. The story is therefore better read as an ancient explanation of how brewing began than as a datable invention.

Date and place

The legend places Yi Di in the age of Yu the Great. An annotation to the Strategies of the Warring States says that the 'daughter of the emperor' was a daughter of Yao or Shun, and cites the Bowuzhi as saying that Yi Di lived in Yu's time; the annotation itself is later and only shows how people of the Han and Wei periods understood the story. The texts leave no verifiable date, and the received Lüshi Chunqiu lists only the four characters 'Yi Di made wine' without saying where. Early texts give no answer to the question of where Yi Di worked; later local narratives supply place names without early evidence, so this essay does not assign a location.

Background: why the ancients credited one person

In the pre-Qin and Han periods there was a convention of 'the sage invents the tool': houses, boats, calendars and bows were each attributed to a single name. The Lüshi Chunqiu follows this pattern, listing 'Yi Di made wine' alongside 'Gao Yuan made houses' and 'Yu Xu made boats'. Such lists answer the question 'who did it first'; they do not offer a chronology.

Brewing is in reality a long process in which damp grain sprouts, grows mould, ferments naturally, and is then observed, imitated and gradually stabilised. Compressing that process into one name made it easier to remember and to worship, and gave wine an ancestor to trace. Yi Di therefore occupies the position of an inventor rather than that of a person with a verifiable life.

Textual layering

Arranged by date, the relevant texts show the story becoming fuller step by step.

Text Date Wording What it adds
Shiben, 'Making Things' Late Warring States to early Han; original lost 'Yi Di first made jiu lao, changing the five flavours' Attributes jiu lao to Yi Di
Lüshi Chunqiu, 'Wu Gong' Late Warring States 'Yi Di made wine' Lists it among inventions, without detail
Strategies of the Warring States, Wei 2 Compiled by Liu Xiang, Western Han 'The emperor's daughter ordered Yi Di to make wine, and it was good; it was presented to Yu ... and he distanced himself from Yi Di and renounced sweet wine' Adds Yu and the warning against sweet wine
Shuowen Jiezi, wine radical Eastern Han 'In ancient times Yi Di made jiu lao; Yu tasted it and found it sweet, and so distanced himself from Yi Di' Retells and fixes the story
Jiugao Jin dynasty 'Some say Yi Di, others say Dukang' Acknowledges that two accounts coexisted

The quotations in the table are taken from the Palace Museum, Chinese Social Sciences Today and the Shaanxi intangible-heritage material. The original Shiben is lost; what survives are reconstructed editions and quotations by later writers, which cannot be used as verbatim Warring States text. The later the text, the fuller the story and the more detail it carries, which is the typical shape of a legend built up in layers.

What jiu lao was

Jiu lao is wine with its lees still in it, liquid and solids mixed and not finely filtered. The Shuowen Jiezi describes Yi Di's product as jiu lao, unlike the clear liquid familiar from later times. Histories of brewing generally treat jiu lao as an unfiltered cloudy liquor made from fermented grain, close to the early forms of yellow wine and rice wine; 'close' is not the same as 'the same process'.

The cultivation of qu, fermentation temperature and pressing all change the finished drink, and no reliable record describes the exact method of early jiu lao. The entries on yellow wine and the history of yellow wine trace this technical line, but equating Yi Di's jiu lao directly with any modern yellow wine remains a leap.

Influence: from wine ancestor to a warning against drink

Yi Di has two later identities. One is the patron of the trade, paired with Dukang; Jiang Tong's Jiugao in the Jin dynasty wrote 'some say Yi Di, others say Dukang'. The other is a negative example: Yu tasted Yi Di's wine, found it sweet, and predicted that 'later generations will surely have rulers who lose their states through wine', so he distanced himself from Yi Di and stopped drinking sweet wine. The story was later cited in arguments for prohibition and moderation and became a fixed saying that wine can ruin a state.

The two identities pull against each other: the same legend makes Yi Di both the founder and the man a ruler turned away from. What it reflects is not one person's fate but an ancient ambivalence towards wine, acknowledging its pleasure while fearing its consequences. Yi Di appears far less often in poetry than Dukang, which also suggests that a legend spreads only when a memorable line later carries it.

Sources and disputes

First, there is no contemporaneous text or archaeological evidence that Yi Di existed. Shang oracle bones and bronze wine vessels show that wine and wine ritual were well developed by the Shang, but that does not prove that one person 'first made wine'.

Second, 'the emperor's daughter' can be read in two ways: either the emperor's daughter ordered Yi Di to make wine, or the emperor's daughter was Yi Di. Neither reading has corroboration, so identity and gender cannot be settled.

Third, archaeology pushes the start of brewing much earlier than the legend. The Jiahu site in Wuyang, Henan, yielded large numbers of pottery vessels in excavations from 1984 to 2001; from 1999 a Chinese-American team analysed residues on the sherds, and a paper published in 2004 concluded that around 7000 BCE the local people were already making a mixed fermented beverage of rice, honey and fruit. Brewing therefore long predates the age of Yu, and Yi Di cannot have been 'the first person to make wine'; the legend is a later retrospective claim on an earlier practice.

Fourth, the question of place has no early record. Later local narratives link Yi Di to particular rivers and villages as part of cultural resource-making, and these cannot be treated as historical sources. Calling such a place 'Yi Di's home town' answers a question that the early texts never answered.

Timeline

  • Around 7000 BCE: pottery residues at Jiahu show a mixed fermented beverage of rice, honey and fruit.
  • Shang dynasty: oracle bones and bronze wine vessels show wine in ritual life.
  • Late Warring States: the Shiben and the Lüshi Chunqiu record that 'Yi Di made wine'.
  • Western Han: the Strategies of the Warring States adds Yu tasting the wine and distancing himself from Yi Di.
  • Eastern Han: the Shuowen Jiezi places Yi Di alongside Dukang in a dictionary.
  • Jin dynasty: Jiang Tong's Jiugao acknowledges 'some say Yi Di, others say Dukang'.
  • 1984 to 2001: excavation of the Jiahu site; residue analysis from 1999, with the paper published in 2004.
