Du Mu

Late Tang poet and prose writer, 803–852; he turns wineshops, wine banners and Apricot Blossom Village into landmarks of travel and city nights, while the widely anthologised "Qingming" is absent from his collected works.

Du Mu (803–852), styled Muzhi, from Wannian in the capital region, was a late Tang poet and prose writer. He came from the Du clan of the capital; his grandfather Du You compiled the Comprehensive Institutions, and the family tradition leaned toward institutions and history. When Du Mu writes wine he rarely writes solitary feeling. He turns the wineshop, the wine banner and the tavern into landmarks of travel and city nights: a shop by the Qinhuai, a flag in a Jiangnan water village, a pointing shepherd boy at Apricot Blossom Village.

Life

Du Mu was born in 803. He passed the metropolitan examination in 828 and in the same year the decree examination for plain speech and direct remonstrance, and was appointed a proofreader in the Hongwen Academy. In 833 he joined the staff of Niu Sengru, military commissioner of Huainan, as secretary, and stayed in Yangzhou about three years. That period later became the material for "Recalling the Past" and for the popular image of him as a careless talent.

He then served as investigating censor at court, was posted out amid the Niu-Li factional struggle, and from 842 was prefect of Huangzhou, later of Chizhou and other places. On the Double Ninth of 844, at Chizhou, he climbed Mount Qi with Zhang Hu and wrote "Climbing Mount Qi on the Double Ninth". In his last years he returned to court as a vice director and historian, went out as prefect of Huzhou in 850, was recalled in 851 as a director of the Ministry of Personnel and drafter of edicts, and in 852 was promoted to drafting official of the Secretariat; he died that winter, aged fifty.

Beyond poetry he wrote a commentary on Sunzi and the essays "Offending Words", "On War" and "On Defence", with concrete views on the military governors, the army system and finance. His "Rhapsody on the Epang Palace" uses the fall of Qin to argue about dynastic collapse and was written in his twenties. To see him only as a gifted romantic is to miss this institutional streak. He is paired with Li Shangyin as the "young Li and Du", and his seven-character quatrains are especially admired.

Wine as an Address

Du Mu's wine usually has an address. It is not the record of a mood, but a marker of where to go and where to stop.

"Mooring on the Qinhuai" reads: "Mist locks the cold water, moonlight locks the sand; at night I moor on the Qinhuai near the wineshops." The wineshop is a specific business on the riverbank and a place where singers performed, scholars gathered and traders rested. The boat stops nearby, and the song comes across the water, so the wineshop becomes the source of a sound. The next line, "the singing girls know nothing of a lost kingdom's grief; across the river they still sing 'The Backyard Flower'", lays a place of consumption over a story of dynastic ruin. The bustle is real and so is the shadow.

"Spring in Jiangnan" reads: "A thousand li of orioles, red against green; wine banners in the wind at water villages and mountain towns." A wine banner was a cloth sign hung out to draw custom, not a modern shop board. In one line, waterways, hills, villages and the wine trade become a map you could walk.

"Recalling the Past" reads: "Down and out on the rivers and lakes, I travelled with wine; slender waists, light as if held in the palm. Ten years, one dream of Yangzhou, and I have won the name of a fickle lover." "Travelling with wine" means carrying drink on the road, not sitting in a shop; the poem turns three years in Yangzhou into a dream that ended too late, and its tone is self-mockery rather than boasting.

"Climbing Mount Qi on the Double Ninth" reads: "In this world one rarely meets a moment for open laughter; chrysanthemums should be stuck all over one's hair before going home. But let me get drunk to honour the festival; no need to regret the setting sun from this height." This is one of his few direct lines about drinking to intoxication: on the Double Ninth, drunkenness becomes an answer to the setting sun.

Principal Works

  • "Mooring on the Qinhuai": a night on the river, a wineshop and "The Backyard Flower".
  • "Spring in Jiangnan": spring scenery and wine banners.
  • "Recalling the Past": memory of Yangzhou, with the famous line about travelling with wine.
  • "Climbing Mount Qi on the Double Ninth": drunkenness as a way of honouring the festival.
  • "Qingming": usually printed under his name, but absent from his collected works.
  • "Mountain Journey": an autumn walk with no wine in it at all.
  • "Rhapsody on the Epang Palace": a celebrated prose rhapsody on ruin.

Textual Evidence: From the Qinhuai to Qingming

"Mooring on the Qinhuai" has only four lines but three layers of space: mist, water, moon and sand in the first; boat and wineshop in the second; the song from the far bank in the third. The poet never enters the shop and never drinks. The wineshop is simply where he ties up, and precisely because he stops there he hears a song that should not be sung. Wine is the means of approach, not the object consumed.

"Qingming" works differently: "In Qingming season the rain falls thick; travellers on the road are stricken in spirit. I ask where a wineshop is; a shepherd boy points far off to Apricot Blossom Village." Four lines contain a traveller, a rainstorm, a question and a pointing finger. The wineshop is a destination for shelter, rest and warmth, and Apricot Blossom Village turns that destination into a nameable place. The liquor trade has reused the name ever since because it is short, musical and visual.

But the poem is not in the Collected Works of Fanzhou. It appears in later compendia and anthologies from the Southern Song onward, and most scholars doubt that Du Mu wrote it, although standard anthologies keep the old attribution. A quotation should say so and separate the poem from the attribution question.

"Mountain Journey" is often misremembered as a drinking poem: "Far up the cold mountain the stone path slants; where white clouds grow there are households. I stop the carriage, taken by the maple woods at dusk; frost-bitten leaves are redder than February flowers." The word is households, not wineshops. One character changes the whole commercial weather of the poem.

Reputation and Disputes

The sharpest dispute is over "Qingming". It is not in the Collected Works, it is not securely recorded in Five Dynasties or Northern Song sources, yet it spread widely through later primers and anthologies. Calling it a Du Mu masterpiece is a fact about reception; calling it certainly his is not supported by the textual record. This page treats it as the former while flagging the latter.

A second dispute comes from Ming dynasty criticism. Yang Shen, in his Remarks on Poetry from the Ascendant Hut, objected to "Spring in Jiangnan": "A thousand li of orioles, who could hear them? A thousand li of green against red, who could see it?" He proposed "ten li" instead. Later writers often rejected the objection, arguing that "a thousand li" is a general view of spring in Jiangnan rather than a measured distance. The critique is still useful: it shows how easily a demand for literal plausibility can flatten a poem's scale.

A third dispute concerns where Apricot Blossom Village was. Chizhou, Fenyang, Nanjing and other places all claim a village of that name, and local gazetteers and tourist narratives each argue their case, but none can be confirmed as the village in the poem. Treating a poetic place name as a locatable coordinate usually produces a new layer of invention.

There is also a judgment about his character. "Recalling the Past" describes his years in Yangzhou among singers and courtesans, and later moralising readers used it to condemn him. But the poem is a literary self-portrait, close to irony, and cannot be read as a page from a diary.

Common Misreadings

First, that "Qingming" is unquestionably Du Mu's. The collection does not include it, attribution is disputed, and the anthologies simply repeat an old ascription.

Second, that Apricot Blossom Village is a place he personally visited. It is more likely a place-name image, claimed by several localities with no decisive evidence.

Third, that a wine banner was a modern advertising board. It was a cloth sign hung outside a shop, moving in the wind, a common way of drawing custom in the old wine trade.

Fourth, that late Tang wine was strong distilled liquor. The main drink was still fermented, and grain wine of the yellow wine lineage was closer to daily drinking.

Fifth, that "Mountain Journey" says "wineshops" where the white clouds grow. It says households; the one-character error turns an autumn landscape into a search for a drink.
