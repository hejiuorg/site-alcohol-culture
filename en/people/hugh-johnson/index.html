Hugh Johnson

A British wine writer born in 1939; he published The World Atlas of Wine in 1971 and has produced Hugh Johnson's Pocket Wine Book since 1977, shaping several generations of readers with maps, vintage tables and regional narrative.

Hugh Johnson (born 1939) is a British wine writer and one of the key figures who brought regional maps into popular wine reading. His name usually appears with two books: The World Atlas of Wine of 1971 and the Pocket Wine Book, updated every year from 1977.

Figure: Hugh Johnson. Photograph: File:DecaturGins.jpg. Credit: Decatur Wine & Spirits, CC BY-SA 3.0 (source page). Category image; not a claim that it shows the exact product, site or event.

Life

He was born on 10 March 1939 and grew up in London. He was educated at Rugby School and King's College, Cambridge, where he read English. In the 1960s he wrote for Condé Nast magazines such as Vogue and House & Garden, later became editor of Wine & Food and wine correspondent of The Sunday Times. In 1966, at the age of 27, he published his first book, Wine, and established his career.

He wrote about both wine and gardening. This cross-over background gives his wine writing a strong sense of geography and landscape. In 2004 he was made a Chevalier of the Ordre National du Mérite, and in 2007 he received an OBE for services to winemaking and horticulture.

Relationship with the wine trade: posts and publishing

Johnson was neither a wine-estate worker nor a laboratory oenologist. His role is closer to that of an interpreter and editor: linking a region's climate, soil and history to the taste of the wine, so that ordinary readers understand why a bottle turned out the way it did.

His career has several verifiable markers: Wine in 1966; The World Atlas of Wine in 1971; Hugh Johnson's Pocket Wine Book every year from 1977; Jancis Robinson joining the revision of the Atlas in 1998 and becoming co-author from the fifth edition in 2001. By 2019 the eighth edition had grown to 416 pages. In 2026 the ninth edition of the Atlas appeared, while the Pocket Wine Book passed to Margaret Rand, with Johnson retired.

The World Atlas of Wine

In 1971 he published The World Atlas of Wine. The first edition had 236 pages and mapped vineyards, villages, soils and administrative boundaries together, with regional notes and representative wines. It changed how wine books were written: readers no longer only read that an estate was good but could see where the land was, what lay beside it and why styles differed.

Later editions of The World Atlas of Wine were revised repeatedly and co-authored with Jancis Robinson. The point of the maps is to turn the abstract "region" into locatable space; once you know two villages are separated by one slope, it is easier to understand why one variety can produce two styles. From the fifth edition in 2001, Robinson took on most of the revision while Johnson remained the founding author and co-author.

The Pocket Wine Book and the annual guide

From 1977 he produced Hugh Johnson's Pocket Wine Book each year. The small volume lists regions and estates with vintage performance, drinking advice and price ranges, and is small enough to fit in a coat pocket. Its value is not depth but the continuity of annual updating: readers can use it as a quick index when buying wine.

The craft of an annual guide is often underestimated. To decide, in limited space, which estates deserve keeping and which vintages need a warning requires long-term tracking and selection. This updating work is closer to the duty of a reference book than to writing a polished column. The 2026 edition was edited by Margaret Rand, which shows that the guide has become an editorial institution that can be handed on.

Writing method and "geographical narrative"

His prose often begins with landscape: a slope, a river, a church, and only then the wine. This takes wine out of tasting jargon and returns it to geography and human activity. He also writes about gardens and trees, and his sensitivity to season, light and soil there is consistent with how he writes about regions.

For readers the advantage is easy access; the risk is mistaking "landscape" for an explanation of "flavour". Geography does affect wine, but flavour also depends on variety, vintage, winemaking and storage. Maps offer clues, not conclusions.

Reputation and controversy

The authority of The World Atlas of Wine is not in doubt, but the first edition had clear limits of coverage. Robinson notes in a retrospective that the 1971 edition had only 236 pages, with just two pages on South America; the "world" of the book was still a Europe-centred world. Later editions expanded to include South America, Asia and Eastern Europe.

The annual guide has the limitation of timeliness. Updating every year means the previous edition quickly becomes dated, and the pocket format allows judgements but not arguments. A reader who treats its scores or recommendations as final will ignore vintage variation, storage and personal taste.

Another point of controversy is authorship. After 2001 the Atlas is a co-authored work by Johnson and Robinson, and the 2026 ninth edition also credits the maps editor Julia Harding. Calling the later editions "Johnson's book alone" contradicts the title page and undervalues the co-authors' work.

Common misreadings

First, treating the atlas as an estate ranking. It is primarily a geographical and regional tool, not a buying guide.

Second, assuming an annual update means a complete rewrite each year. Continuity matters more than novelty in a reference book.

Third, treating a British regional perspective as the only one. He knows the European tradition well, but reading New World wines still needs local material.

Fourth, repeating the claim that he died in 2023. As of 2026 he is alive and has retired from editing the annual guide; some second-hand sources confuse him with a namesake or with old news.

How to use his books

First find your bottle's village on the map, then read the climate and soil notes for that region, then check the vintage table. After three steps you at least know what the wine "should" be like. If it tastes very different, go back to craft and vintage for the reason. The greatest use of a reference book is to help you ask better questions.
