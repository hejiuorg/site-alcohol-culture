Jerry Thomas

An American bartender, commonly dated 1825 to 1885 (some sources give 1830). In 1862 he published How to Mix Drinks in New York, turning word-of-mouth recipes into a book, and was known as the Professor. The Blue Blazer gave bartending a theatrical side, though he did not invent the classic cocktails.

Jerry Thomas (1825-1885) was one of the best-known American bartenders of the nineteenth century. His fame rests on two things: a drinks book published in 1862 and a set of flaming mixing routines. The book fixed recipes in a stable written form; the routines turned the bartender from a server behind a counter into a performer on a stage.

A man surrounded by his own legend

Most stories about Thomas can be traced to his own book, newspaper advertisements and obituaries, and rarely to independent evidence. He called himself the Professor and was happy to be written up as a character; nineteenth-century American bars were good at making characters. Any biography should keep "he said", "the papers said" and "the archive proves" in separate columns.

His birth year is the first example. Common references give 30 October 1830, while other research using parish and genealogical material pushes it back to 1825. This profile uses 1825 and keeps 1830 in the text rather than hiding the disagreement.

Life

  • Born in 1825 (some sources say 1830) in Sackets Harbor, New York; the exact date is uncertain.
  • Learned bar work as a teenager in New York State and Connecticut and is said to have run a bar on his own before he was twenty.
  • Went to California around 1849 with the gold rush and worked as head bartender at the El Dorado gambling saloon in San Francisco.
  • Returned east in the 1850s and worked at Planters' House in St. Louis and later at the Metropolitan Hotel in New York.
  • Published How to Mix Drinks; or, The Bon-Vivant's Companion in New York in 1862, printed by Dick & Fitzgerald.
  • Issued a revised and enlarged edition, The Bar-Tender's Guide, in 1876.
  • Died in New York on 15 December 1885 after a stroke.

His actual role in the drinks trade

His job was always behind the bar or running a bar, not owning a distillery or a wholesale house. That position shapes his contribution: he did not invent a drink so much as turn bar practice into a text that could be copied.

The work can be listed concretely: running the bar and its output at hotel saloons in San Francisco and New York; training assistants; buying spirits and equipment; adjusting recipes to customers' taste; and turning daily practice into a book. After 1862 he kept working in bars and at times ran his own place. The career path is close to the modern one of bartender, bar owner and author.

The 1862 book

How to Mix Drinks sorted the recipes then passed on by word of mouth into categories, including punches, cocktails, sours, fizzes, juleps, smashes, cobblers and toddies, with quantities and steps. It was not the first English book to mention mixed drinks, but it was one of the most influential early American books on the subject, and "the bartender now had a manual" mattered more than any single recipe.

The point was standardisation. Before it, the same drink could be made differently in different cities and bars; with a text, recipes could be copied, compared and argued over. Today's cocktail books, bar training and recipe archives all go back to this practice of writing handwork down.

The Whiskey Cocktail recipe in the book is now seen as the direct ancestor of the Old-Fashioned: spirits, sugar, water and bitters, which is the basic structure of the older drink. The name "Old-Fashioned" itself became current only in the 1880s, so the later name should not be projected back onto the 1862 text.

The Blue Blazer and performance

Thomas's most cited creation is the Blue Blazer. Whisky and boiling water are poured back and forth between two metal mugs and set alight, producing a trail of fire, before sugar and lemon peel are added. It is said to have been his signature at the El Dorado in San Francisco.

The drink gave mixing a circus quality and became one of the spiritual sources of the later cocktail revival and flair bartending. It is not a technique for everyday use: open flame, boiling water and strong spirits appear together, and trying it at home carries a serious risk of burns and fire. Its value is historical and theatrical rather than that of a classic recipe everyone must learn.

The Professor and professional identity

The title "Professor" expressed respect for skill and also carried the marketing flavour of nineteenth-century American bars. It marks a change: the bartender began to be seen as someone with specialised knowledge rather than a server who handed over drinks. Bars therefore became places where technique was displayed and recipes circulated, which fits the history of taverns.

For trade history the title matters more than any single drink. It signals the formation of an occupation with textbooks, apprentices, signature moves and performances that could be imitated.

Assessment and disputes: what the sources can prove

First, the birth year and early career are disputed. Both 1825 and 1830 have support, and the exact years and names of his posts do not fully agree across sources.

Second, claims of priority are hard to verify. Whether he invented the Blue Blazer, or first made a particular drink at a particular bar, often depends on his own later recollections and on newspaper repetition; David Wondrich, reconstructing his life in Imbibe! (2007), qualified a number of the familiar stories.

Third, the book is a compilation, not a complete original oeuvre. Many recipes came from colleagues and earlier publications, and Thomas's contribution was selection, classification and record. Mistaking "collected" for "invented" is the easiest error for later readers to make.

Fourth, his relation to titles such as "father of American bartending" is a later attribution. Labels travel well but can obscure the collective nature of the nineteenth-century American bar.

Common misreadings

First, taking "Professor" as an academic degree. It was a title within bar circles, not a university post.

Second, crediting every drink in the book to him. His book is a compilation, and many recipes came from colleagues and earlier publications.

Third, treating the Blue Blazer as an everyday technique. It is a performance with a high safety risk.

Fourth, treating 1862 as the year cocktails were born. Cocktails existed before; the book merely wrote them down.

Fifth, treating the birth year as settled fact. Since 1825 and 1830 both circulate, a citation should state which it follows.
