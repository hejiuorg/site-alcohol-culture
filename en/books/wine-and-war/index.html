Wine and War

A narrative history of how French winegrowers, merchants and estates tried to preserve their vineyards, stocks and trade during the Nazi occupation of 1940–1944. Wine appears here as both a commodity and a symbol of national identity, and therefore as an object contested by occupiers, the Vichy regime, resisters and ordinary families.

The English original carries a long subtitle, roughly "the French, the Nazis, and the battle for France's greatest treasure". It is not a book about battles. It is about how growers, merchants and estates tried to preserve their vineyards, stocks and business during the German occupation of France from 1940 to 1944. Wine is both a commodity and part of the national self-image, which is why it became an object repeatedly contested by the occupiers, the Vichy regime, resisters and ordinary families.

What this book is

This is a narrative history for general readers, not an academic monograph. It weaves interviews, family memories, memoirs and archival material into a series of stories: someone hides valuable wine in a wall, someone uses false labels to deal with requisition, someone negotiates with German officers in a cellar, and someone is accused after the war of having collaborated. The France it presents is not a tidy myth of resistance but a real society of compromise, fear, ingenuity and luck.

Its themes have three layers. The first is economic: wine was an important French export and source of tax revenue; the occupiers wanted it, and the growers needed to survive. The second is cultural: wine was treated as "the essence of France", so protecting it meant protecting a way of life. The third is moral: under extreme conditions, the line between preserving a family business and collaborating with the enemy was not always clear.

Authors and editions

The authors are Don Kladstrup and Petie Kladstrup, both American journalists who lived in France for many years; their style favours people and scenes over statistics and theory. The original was published by Broadway Books in 2001, and later editions and translations followed. Subtitles, chapter arrangements and illustrations may differ between editions, so the edition should be cited.

It is important to say what the book is not: it is neither a complete history of French wine nor a military history of the Second World War. Its time frame is the occupation, and its geography is mainly the major French regions; pre-war wine, post-war reconstruction and the German or Italian side are treated only as background. If you want want a fuller history of the occupation should read academic work alongside it.

Core argument

First, wine in wartime was not a luxury but a strategic resource. The occupiers needed it for the economy and for supply, and the growers needed it to live; neither side wanted the vineyards to stop. Second, wine was a vehicle of identity politics. Protecting a cellar, concealing stock or refusing requisition was often described as "defending France", but the same act could also be simple commercial self-preservation. Third, resistance and collaboration are not a binary. The people in the book often do two things at once: deal with the occupiers while secretly protecting stock; complain about the Vichy government while depending on its rationing system.

These three points make the book more complicated than a heroic story. It does not deny resistance, and it does not turn everyone into a hero; it is more interested in how ordinary people made choices under pressure, and how those choices were remembered and judged after the war.

Structure and how to read it

The book moves roughly chronologically while following regions, families and individuals. The reader accompanies different winegrowing families through requisition, rationing, the black market, concealment and liberation. The chapters are not tightly argued; they are more like a set of connected reports. It therefore helps to read them as a collection of stories while supplying one's own timeline and map.

A suggested approach: read the preface and the early occupation chapters first to establish the chronology; then read by region, noticing how German agencies, Vichy officials and local networks differed; finally read the liberation and post-war reckoning, where memory is sorted out. When you meet a specific figure, price or casualty count, do not quote the book directly; check archives or academic research.

Verifiable factual boundaries

The book supports the "general process" and "individual experience": occupation, requisition, hidden wine, the black market and liberation all existed, and many winegrowing families have similar memories. It cannot on its own support precise stock figures, transaction prices, the exact words of a conversation or the assertion that "someone certainly did something". Interview material is shaped by memory, position and time, and an author may rearrange a scene for narrative effect.

When citing the book, it is best treated as a combination of oral history and popular narrative, not as archival evidence. To use a story in serious research, go back to the interviews, memoirs or local archives on which it rests; to understand the atmosphere of the wine trade under occupation, the book is a good entry point.

Limits and controversies

First, the narrative favours drama. Heroic stories of hidden wine are memorable, while quiet compromise, everyday collaboration and grey areas may be compressed. Second, the perspective is mainly that of French growers; the complexity of German occupiers, Vichy officials and ordinary consumers is less developed. Third, oral history is not fully reliable: memory beautifies, omits and rewrites, and the authors may add imagined detail when reconstructing a scene. Fourth, the book has a clear tone of sympathy and national narrative, and the reader needs to keep some distance.

Fifth, it does not discuss the public-health side of alcohol consumption, nor does it analyse the structural changes in the French wine trade after the war. It is better described as a portrait of people in the wartime wine business than as a complete history of the industry.

Who should read it

  • General readers interested in the Second World War and the occupation: a highly readable entry point.
  • Lovers of French wine and travellers to the regions: it explains why estates, cellars and family succession matter so much.
  • Researchers of oral history and memory: a case study of how memory is told.
  • Wine professionals: it shows the effect of war on stock, channels and brands, but it is not a source of business data.
  • Academic readers: find archives and monographs separately; use this book as background and leads.
