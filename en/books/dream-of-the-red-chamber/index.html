Dream of the Red Chamber is a chapter novel by Cao Xueqin. It circulated in manuscript form from the mid-18th century, and only in 1791 did a 120-chapter edition appear, prepared by Cheng Weiyuan and Gao E. Wine in this novel almost never appears alone: it has a name, a temperature, someone to pour it, and a rule about how much one should drink. Reading these passages means reading the daily order of the Jia household, and also reading where each character stands within that order.

What kind of novel this is

Cao Xueqin was born around 1715 and died in 1763. His family had once been prominent and later declined, an experience often thought to mirror the rise and fall of the Jia family in the novel. The story centres on Jia Baoyu, Lin Daiyu, Xue Baochai and their loves and fates, and traces the decline of the household through an extremely dense texture of everyday detail.

The received text usually runs to 120 chapters. The first 80 are generally attributed to Cao Xueqin, while the authorship and making of the last 40 remain disputed: they may have been continued or arranged by Gao E, or may rest on surviving drafts by Cao. This edition problem directly affects reading, because the two parts differ in character voice, plot density and handling of detail.

When you meet a drink name, vessel or drinking game, first establish which chapter it comes from and whether it belongs to the first 80 or the last 40. That is safer than treating it at once as a record of Qing drinking customs. The novel is literature, not a survey of the drinks trade.

Wine in the daily life of the Jia household

Wine appears in many ordinary scenes: birthdays, New Year, flower viewing, receiving guests, farewells and poetry gatherings. It is both a courtesy owed to a guest and a medium that lets characters relax. An elder offers wine and a junior rises to receive it; a maid pours, but the mistress may pour for herself; wine is warmed in cold weather, and a frail person drinks only half a cup.

These details show that wine in the Jia household is not a drink taken at will but a social act embedded in hierarchy, age and intimacy. Who pours for whom, who raises the cup first and who is pressed to drink often reveal the relationship better than the drink itself.

That is why drunkenness matters in the novel. Being drunk is not only a physical state but a moment when a character leaves the rules behind: someone speaks the truth through drink, someone loses decorum, someone falls asleep among flowers. Wine loosens the grip of propriety a little, and in doing so makes propriety more visible.

Yellow wine and Huiquan wine

In chapter 38, at the crab banquet, Lin Daiyu pours herself half a cup and finds that it is “yellow wine”. She then says that eating crab has left her chest slightly sore and that she needs hot shaojiu. This short passage places yellow wine on the ordinary banquet table: it is the wine already at hand, mild and suited to a meal, but not the hot, strong drink she wants at that moment.

Huiquan wine appears more than once in the book. In chapter 16, Xifeng invites Nanny Zhao to taste “the Huiquan wine your son brought back”, adding “only do not drink too much”. In chapter 62, Fangguan says that at home she used to drink “two or three jin of good Huiquan wine”. Huiquan is in Wuxi, and Huiquan wine is often explained as a famous Jiangnan wine made with water from the Huiquan spring, but the novel gives no details of its method, and we should not supply a recipe for it.

In type, these drinks belong closer to the lineage of yellow wine: grain ferments, usually lower in alcohol than distilled spirits, suited to warming and to food. The “yellow wine” and “Huiquan wine” of the novel are not necessarily the same drink, but both belong among the fermented wines common at the tables of well-off households of the time.

Shaojiu and acacia-flower infusion

Again in chapter 38, Daiyu asks for “hot shaojiu”, and Baoyu orders someone to warm “a pot of the wine infused with acacia flowers”. Shaojiu is a general term for distilled or strong liquor, not the same category as yellow wine; acacia-flower infusion is a medicinal-style preparation in which acacia blossoms are steeped in liquor. The two pieces of information side by side show different drinks and different drinking methods sharing one table.

The action of warming the pot is crucial. Warming wine was not only a matter of taste; it also belonged to contemporary ideas about the nature of alcohol, the body and cold. Daiyu is frail and feels sore after eating crab, so she wants something hot and strong; Baoyu chooses the acacia infusion, which carries a note of care. Wine here is drink, medicine and affection at once.

Read the passage with proportion: the novel records a character's feeling and a household's experience, not clinical advice. Treating “crab with hot shaojiu” as a dietary guideline today crosses the boundary between literature and medicine. If you are interested in the logic of pairing, see the basic principles in food pairing, but do not use a novel as nutritional evidence.

Drinking games and banquet play

Drinking games are an important part of banquets in the novel. In chapter 40, “Golden Yuanyang Thrice Announces the Ivory-Tile Tokens” combines tiles, lines of verse and seating order; whoever cannot answer must drink. Granny Liu's reply about “a great fire burning the caterpillars” creates one of the book's famous comic scenes. The game is not only play; it tests quickness, learning and status at the same time.

In chapter 62, at Baoyu's birthday, the table has shefu, a guessing game, and thumb wrestling. Shi Xiangyun, having been fined two extra cups, falls asleep among the peonies. The games turn a meal into regulated liveliness; the more elaborate the rules, the more they reveal the participants' wit and character. Xiangyun's boldness, Daiyu's sensitivity and Baochai's steadiness all show in how they play and how they drink.

In chapter 28, at Feng Ziying's table, Xue Pan takes a turn at the drinking game and speaks crudely while the others laugh. The same form produces entirely different effects in different mouths; this is precisely how the novel uses drink to write character. Whether a game is elegant or vulgar is decided not by the words alone but by the relationships present.

Wine and character

Jia Baoyu's attitude to wine is contradictory: he enjoys the liveliness of a banquet and dislikes the crudeness and pressure that can go with it. Fetching the acacia infusion for Daiyu shows tenderness; avoiding certain social occasions shows impatience with ceremony. Wine is not a single label attached to him.

Wang Xifeng uses wine as a tool of management. She can press a guest to drink, direct the service, carry a banquet and also remind others to stop. In her hands wine is lubrication for power and human relations, not a private appetite.

Characters such as Xue Pan and Granny Liu often produce comedy by failing to drink according to the rules. The first is crude and blustering, the second brings country wit, and both are magnified through games and drunken behaviour. The novel does not merely laugh at them; it also lets the reader see how the rules of a noble banquet are taken apart by outsiders.

Wine rites and moderation

The wine rites of the Jia household are not abstract; they sit in concrete actions. An elder offers wine and a junior receives it; a maid pours and the mistress may dismiss her; guests are welcomed with wine and cold weather calls for warming. Wine is the sincerity of hospitality and also a marker of status.

At the same time, reminders to drink less recur. In chapter 16, when Xifeng asks Nanny Zhao to taste the Huiquan wine, the latter answers, “only do not drink too much”. Such remarks are common at a banquet and show a clear contemporary caution about excess. The rite does not encourage heavy drinking; it cares more about drinking “according to the rules”.

This “regulated liveliness” is the key to understanding the household's drinking culture. The novel writes both the pleasure of wine and its disorder, both the abundance of the table and the awkwardness of drunkenness. Read both sides, and Dream of the Red Chamber does not collapse into a guide to aristocratic eating and drinking.

How to read the wine passages

First check the chapter and the edition. The first 80 and the last 40 chapters may differ in detail, and a character's drinking habits need not be perfectly consistent across the whole book. When you meet a drink name, note the chapter first and then consult an annotated edition rather than piecing it together from memory.

Then ask what wine is doing in the scene: hospitality, a birthday, a farewell, a game, or care for a sick person. Different functions call for different drinks and different ways of serving them. Put “yellow wine”, “shaojiu”, “Huiquan wine” and “acacia-flower infusion” back into their scenes, and the author's sense of proportion becomes visible.

Finally watch the characters' responses. Who pours, who presses others to drink, who takes only half a cup and who collapses drunk are all clues to personality and relationship. Treat the wine passages as isolated records of custom and you lose what the novel does best: making character appear through concrete action.
